Chapter 25

Scaling across products, brands and platforms

A design system that works beautifully for one product and one team gets tested in new ways when the company grows: a second brand, a mobile app, an acquisition, a new business unit with its own ideas. This chapter is about stretching the system without breaking it.

Read this if

Your system needs to serve several brands, several platforms or several business units, or you're planning for that future.

What you'll leave with
  1. How to support several brands and platforms from one source of decisions.
  2. How "families of systems" work, and when you need one.
  3. Governance and funding models that work across business units.

25.1What changes when you scale

Scaling a design system isn't just "the same thing, bigger". New kinds of difference appear: different brands that must look different, different platforms that must feel native, and different business units with different priorities and budgets. Each one tempts teams to start their own system.

ScenarioOne group, three brands, three systems
The situation

A financial group runs three consumer brands: a bank, an insurance company and a lending app. Each brand has its own design team, and each built its own design system.

What goes wrong

Three teams maintain three date pickers, three accessibility fixes, three token pipelines. When a regulation changes the rules for how fees must be displayed, it's implemented three times, differently.

What scaling well looks like

One shared core (behaviour, accessibility, structure, foundations) with a brand layer on top. Each brand still looks like itself; the hard work is done once.

The guiding principle for everything in this chapter is simple: share what should be the same, and make what should differ easy to differ. Behaviour, accessibility and structure should almost always be shared. Visual identity, and sometimes platform conventions, should be allowed to vary, in controlled ways.

25.2Several brands from one system

Chapter 07 introduced brand tokens, and Chapter 20 showed how component tokens let a brand restyle specific components. Put together, they make a multi-brand system practical. Here's what usually stays shared and what varies.

LayerShared across brandsVaries by brand
BehaviourKeyboard use, focus, announcements, validation logicNothing
StructureComponent anatomy, props, patternsRarely; only through agreed extensions
Semantic tokensThe names of the jobsWhich values the names point to
ColourThe lightness curve and contrast rulesBrand hues, and sometimes neutral tints
TypographyThe roles (heading, body, label) and accessibility rulesFont families, sometimes the scale
Shape and motionThe token namesCorner rounding, shadows, animation style
Imagery and voiceAccessibility rulesIllustrations, photography, tone of voice

The result is that a new brand is mostly a new set of values, not a new system. Because the lightness curve is shared (Chapter 07), every brand's colours meet the same contrast rules automatically, even when their hues are completely different. A regulation change to how fees are shown becomes one pattern update, inherited by all three brands.

[[prac]] Test with the most different brand first

When building a multi-brand system, design the first components against your two most different brands at once, not one brand first. It's much easier to find the right flexibility points while building than to add them afterwards.

25.3Several platforms from one source

Web, iPhone and Android each have their own conventions. Apple's guidelines and Google's Material Design describe different navigation, different controls and different text sizes.[[c:hig-layout,m3-structure]] A good multi-platform system respects those conventions while keeping the brand and the experience recognisably the same.

Decide to…UsuallyExamples
ShareBrand, foundations, content, component names and purposesColours, voice, "primary button", error message formulas
AdaptThings where the platform has strong conventionsNavigation patterns, date pickers, switches, text sizes, haptics
Keep separateThings that only exist on one platformWidgets, watch faces, keyboard shortcuts on desktop

A useful test: would a regular user of this platform be surprised by how this behaves? If yes, adapt to the platform. If no, share it. People expect an iPhone app to feel like an iPhone app, while still recognising your brand.

25.4Families of systems

At a certain size, one system can't sensibly hold everything. A bank's customer app, its internal operations tools and its marketing site have such different needs that forcing them into one library makes it worse for all three. The answer many large companies arrive at is a family of systems: a shared core, with specialised systems built on top of it.

A family of systems only works with a few clear rules. Everyone uses the core tokens, so the brand and accessibility baseline stay consistent. Specialised systems build on core components rather than copying them, so fixes flow outwards. Each specialised system has a named owning team, usually the one closest to its users. And things move into the core when they're needed widely, following the rule of two from Chapter 05. Without these rules, a family of systems slowly becomes the "22 separate systems" problem again.

25.5Governance and funding across business units

When several business units depend on one system, two questions become political very quickly: who decides, and who pays. Answering them openly, early, prevents a lot of quiet resentment.

Who decides

Most large organisations settle on a small council: the system lead plus a design and engineering representative from each major business unit, meeting on a regular rhythm for decisions that affect everyone (foundations, breaking changes, the roadmap). Day-to-day decisions stay with the system team and contributors, as described in Chapter 05. The council is for the few decisions that genuinely need everyone in the room.

Scenario"Our unit wants to opt out"
The situation

One business unit complains the system is too slow for its needs and asks to build its own.

What to do

Treat it as feedback first: what's missing, and why is it slow? Often the answer is an extension owned by that unit, built on the core, with a fast lane for their needs.

Why

Opting out fully costs the unit the shared accessibility, brand and maintenance work, and costs everyone consistency. A well-supported extension usually gives them the speed they want without either cost.

25.6Legacy products and long migrations

At scale, there's always an older product that can't adopt the new system quickly: different technology, no team assigned, or a complete rewrite planned "next year". Don't let these products hold the system back, and don't leave them behind entirely.

  1. Tokens first

    Ship the token values (as CSS variables or a theme file) into the legacy product. Colours, type and spacing align quickly, and the product looks part of the family (Chapter 22, case 4).

  2. Replace the most visible parts

    Navigation, buttons and forms, where users notice inconsistency most. Replace them as screens are touched, not all at once.

  3. Measure the whole portfolio

    Track adoption per product on one simple dashboard, so leadership sees progress across the whole company, not just in the newest products.

  4. Agree an end state

    For each legacy product, decide openly: full adoption, tokens only, or retirement. A clear plan is better than an endless "someday".

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Share behaviour, accessibility and structure; let brand and platform conventions vary in controlled ways.
  2. One token source can feed every brand and platform; components are built natively with the same names.
  3. At large scale, use a family of systems: a shared core plus specialised systems with named owners.
  4. Decide openly who decides and who pays, and bring legacy products along with tokens first.
