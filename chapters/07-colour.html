Chapter 07

Colour systems

Colour is the foundation teams get wrong most often, and the one where getting it right pays off fastest. We'll go step by step, from how colours are described, to building a palette, to making dark mode a simple switch. Every value in this chapter is calculated, not guessed.

Read this if

You're creating or fixing a colour palette, planning dark mode or a second brand, or your accessibility audits keep flagging text that's hard to read.

What you'll leave with
  1. Why the way you describe colour matters, and why we recommend a model called OKLCH.
  2. A repeatable method for building a palette, with a complete worked example.
  3. A way of naming colours that makes dark mode and new brands a settings change.

07.1Why colour goes wrong

Let's start with a story we've seen in some form at almost every company we've audited.

Scenario340 greys and no way to switch them
The situation

A five-year-old product finally commits to dark mode. An engineer searches the code for colour values to see how big the job is.

What they find

340 different colour values, most of them greys that are almost, but not quite, the same. Some are for text, some for borders, some for backgrounds. Nothing in the code says which is which.

Why it matters

To build dark mode, someone has to look at every one of those 340 values in context and decide what it should become. A two-sprint feature turns into a two-quarter project.

Nobody set out to create 340 greys. A designer picked a grey that looked right for a divider. Another picked a slightly lighter one for a caption. An engineer approximated one from a screenshot. Each choice was reasonable. Together they're unmanageable.

There's a second, quieter problem too: text that's hard to read. Pale grey text looks elegant in a design file on a bright monitor. On a laptop in sunlight, or for someone with less-than-perfect eyesight, it disappears. WebAIM's 2025 scan of the world's top million home pages found text with too little contrast on 79.1% of them, making it the single most common accessibility problem on the web.[[c:webaim-2025]] [[doc]]

A colour system solves both problems. It gives you a small, deliberate set of colours, each checked for readability, each named by the job it does. The rest of this chapter shows you how to build one.

07.2How colour is described

To a computer, a colour is just numbers, and there are several ways of writing those numbers down. They're called colour models. You've probably seen some of them: #CC3C00 or rgb(204, 60, 0). The model you use matters more than you'd think, because it decides which questions are easy to answer. For a design system, the most important question is: is this colour lighter or darker than that one, and by how much? Readability, hierarchy and hover effects all depend on it.

ModelDescribes colour asGood forThe catch
RGB / HEXAmounts of red, green and blue lightHow screens actually work; every tool understands itUnreadable to humans. Nobody can look at #CC3C00 and know how to make it 10% lighter.
HSL / HSVHue (which colour), saturation, lightnessPicking colours by feel; most colour pickers use itIts "lightness" isn't what your eyes see. A yellow and a blue with the same HSL lightness look completely different.
OKLCHLightness (0 to 1), chroma (how colourful), hue (which colour)Building palettes and themesSome combinations can't be shown on normal screens, and its lightness isn't exactly the same as the official readability measure. Both are manageable (see below).

"Lightness" that matches your eyes

The big difference is that OKLCH is a perceptual model. It was designed so that the numbers match what people actually see. It's based on a colour space called Oklab, published by Björn Ottosson in 2020,[[c:ottosson-oklab]] and it's now built into CSS, the language that styles websites, as oklch().[[c:css-color-4]] [[std]]

The easiest way to see why this matters is to look. Below, the top row shows six colours that HSL says are all equally light. The grey strip under each one shows how light it really looks.

The three dials of OKLCH

You can think of OKLCH as three dials. Turn one and the other two stay put.

Lightness L 0 → 1

How light it looks, from black (0) to white (1). This is the dial that controls readability.

Chroma C 0 → ~0.37

How colourful it is. Zero is grey. The maximum depends on the hue and the lightness.

Hue H 0° → 360°

Which colour family, as an angle around a wheel. Every swatch in this row looks equally light.

Why OKLCH is so useful for a design system

Because the dials are independent and lightness matches your eyes, a lot of hard problems get easy. You can say "shade 700 of every colour is this light" and it will be true for blue, red and green alike. You can swap one brand colour for another by turning only the hue dial, without changing how light anything is, so readability doesn't break. Palettes become a recipe rather than a pile of hand-picked swatches. Gradients look cleaner. And OKLCH can describe the more vivid colours modern screens can show. It's supported in CSS and in the shared token format,[[c:css-color-4,dtcg-format]] and Tailwind CSS, one of the most widely used styling frameworks, switched its whole default palette to OKLCH in version 4.0.[[c:tailwind-v4]] [[doc]]

Two things to watch out for

1. Some combinations can't be displayed. Screens can only show a certain range of colours. Very colourful shades that are also very light or very dark often fall outside that range. Good tools quietly turn down the chroma dial until the colour fits. Our palette generator does this and marks each colour where it happened.

2. OKLCH lightness isn't the official readability score. The accessibility guidelines measure contrast with a different formula.[[c:wcag-contrast]] Equal OKLCH lightness gives similar readability across colours, not identical. You still check every pairing; 07.4 shows how close it gets.

07.3Building a palette, step by step

Step 1: start from your brand colour, but don't use it for everything

Most palettes start from a brand colour, and the first surprise is usually that the exact brand colour can't be used for text or for buttons with white text on them. Layer One's own orange, #ff6b3d, is a perfect example. On white it has a contrast ratio of just 2.83 to 1, well below the 4.5 to 1 the accessibility guidelines require for normal text. On the dark ink background, it reaches a comfortable 6.87 to 1. [[std]]

The fix isn't to change the brand. It's to treat the brand colour as a starting point for a family of shades, then use different shades for different jobs. The exact brand orange stays for big brand moments like the logo and illustrations. A deeper shade from the same family is used for buttons and links on light backgrounds. In the palette below, the brand family is generated from #ff6b3d, and shade 700 becomes the main action colour.

Step 2: add supporting colours for specific jobs

A product needs far fewer colours than a brand guideline suggests. Most products need one brand colour, a family of greys, and a few colours that signal status:

ColourIts jobRule of thumb
BrandMain actions, selected items, brand momentsNever reused to mean "error" or "success"
Neutral (greys)Text, backgrounds, borders: most of the screenVery slightly tinted, so it doesn't feel lifeless
RedErrors, destructive actions like "Delete"Clearly different from the brand colour
GreenSuccess, confirmationAlways paired with an icon or words
AmberWarnings, pending statesUse dark text on amber, not white
BlueLinks, information, keyboard focus outlinesCan double as the brand colour if your brand is blue

One important rule: colour should never be the only way to tell people something. Colour blindness is common, screens and lighting vary, and WCAG 2.2 requires that colour isn't the only visual signal.[[c:wcag22]] [[std]] So a red error message also gets an icon and clear words.

Watch for clashes between your brand and your status colours, too. Ledgerly's orange sits at hue 37° and its error red at 25°, only 12° apart. That's common with warm brands, and it means colour alone could never tell "main action" from "delete". The answer isn't a different colour. It's design: delete buttons get clear labels and a confirmation step, and errors always come with an icon.

Step 3: build each colour into a family of shades

New wordRampA family of shades of one colour, from very light to very dark, numbered like 50, 100, 200 … 950.

Each colour becomes a ramp: a set of shades numbered from very light (50) to very dark (950). The clever part, and the reason we use OKLCH, is that each shade number has the same lightness in every colour. Blue 700, red 700 and grey 700 are all equally light (L ≈ 0.485). So the number itself tells you something useful: "anything 700 or darker is safe for text on white".

Notice that the shades are bunched up at both ends. That's on purpose. Interfaces need lots of very light tints (backgrounds, hover states, selected rows) and several very dark shades (text, dark-mode surfaces), but only a few mid-tones. Other well-known systems take a similar purpose-driven approach: Radix Colors uses 12 steps, each with a stated job from "app background" to "high-contrast text",[[c:radix-scale]] and Google's Material Design generates families of tones from a key colour.[[c:m3-color]] [[doc]]

Step 4: shape the colourfulness

Colourfulness should peak in the middle of the ramp and fade towards both ends. Very light shades that are very colourful look neon; very dark shades that are very colourful look muddy. That's the "colourfulness" row in the table above. Where a shade would be too colourful for screens to show, the generator turns the chroma dial down until it fits, keeping lightness the same so readability stays predictable.

07.4Making sure people can read it

New wordContrast ratioHow different two colours are in brightness, from 1:1 (identical) to 21:1 (black on white). Higher is easier to read.

Readability is measured with a contrast ratio: a number that compares how bright the text is with how bright the background is. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 set three thresholds you'll use all the time:[[c:wcag-contrast,wcag-nontext]] [[std]]

4.5:1The minimum for normal text. (7:1 for the stricter AAA level.)
3:1The minimum for large text: about 24px, or about 19px if bold.
3:1The minimum for the edges of buttons and inputs, focus outlines and meaningful icons.

Because every ramp uses the same lightness curve, contrast behaves almost identically across colours. The chart below proves it: six colours, six lines that nearly sit on top of each other.

The light grey text trap

The most common readability mistake is "secondary" text, like captions, timestamps and helper text, set in a pale grey because it looks refined. It's the main reason so many websites fail contrast checks. Fix it once, at the source: Ledgerly's secondary text colour points to grey 700, which reaches 5.99:1 on the background. Nothing lighter is allowed for text, and because every component uses that one named colour, every screen passes.

What about APCA?

You may hear about APCA, a newer way of measuring contrast that takes text size and weight into account and has been explored for future versions of the guidelines.[[c:apca]] Radix Colors, for example, tunes its text shades with it.[[c:radix-scale]] It's promising, but today the WCAG 2 ratio is the official requirement. Use WCAG 2 to pass, and APCA as extra guidance if you like.

07.5Naming colours by their job

A palette is a box of paints. A colour system is the set of labels that says what each paint is for. This is the single most important idea in this chapter, so let's take it slowly.

There are two kinds of name. The first kind describes the colour: "blue 600", "grey 950". The second kind describes the job: "link text", "page background", "error message". Components only ever use the second kind. The job names point to the colour names.

New wordSemantic tokenA colour named by its job ("text-secondary") rather than its appearance ("grey-700"). It points to a raw colour.

The design systems world has formal names for these levels. Colour names are called primitive tokens (the raw ingredients). Job names are called semantic tokens ("semantic" just means "about meaning"). Some systems add a third level of component tokens for settings specific to one component. Every mature public design system we know of separates raw values from their jobs, even if they use different words for it.[[c:atlassian-tokens,carbon-themes,spectrum-tokens,slds-tokens]] [[doc]]

Why bother with two names?

Dark mode becomes a switch

Dark mode just changes which paint each job points to. No component changes.

Fixes happen in one place

If error text needs to be darker, you change one pointer, not every red in the product.

Checks are simple

"Is secondary text readable?" is one check, not an audit of every grey ever used for text.

The palette stays tidy

People choose from a short list of jobs, not 66 swatches, so new random colours stop appearing.

Do you need the third level?

Component settings add another layer to name, document and maintain. Use them if you support several brands or white-labelled versions that need to restyle specific components, or if one component genuinely needs to differ from the normal rule. Otherwise, components can use job names directly. Many teams start with two levels and add the third only when they need it. [[prac]]

Naming the jobs

Pick a simple naming pattern so that people can guess names they haven't seen. That's Rule #3 of how we work: name things so people can guess them. This pattern works for most products: what kind of thing, then its role, then (if needed) a variation and a state.

color.bg.surface                      // backgrounds: canvas, surface, subtle
color.text.secondary                   // text: primary, secondary, disabled, link
color.border.strong                    // borders: default, strong
color.action.primary.bg.hover         // interactive: role, part, state
color.feedback.danger.text            // status: danger, warning, success, info

07.6Colours that respond: hover, press and focus

Buttons, links and other interactive elements need to change colour when you hover over them, press them, or reach them with the keyboard. There are two common ways to handle this.

Approach A — step along the ramp

Move one shade darker

Hover goes one shade darker in light mode (brand 700 → 800) and one lighter in dark mode. Simple, easy to check for readability, and every state has a name.

DefaultHoverPressedFocus
Approach B — add a see-through layer

Lay a tint on top

A faint, see-through layer is placed over the button on hover or press. Material Design uses this approach. It works on any colour and needs fewer names.

Default+ hover layer+ press layer

Focus deserves special attention. When someone moves through a page with the keyboard (many people do, and some have to), the focus outline shows where they are. It must contrast at least 3:1 with what's around it.[[c:wcag-nontext]] Give it its own named colour (Ledgerly uses blue 600, which reaches 4.28:1 on the background) rather than reusing the brand colour, which may not stand out against the button it surrounds.

Disabled elements are exempt from contrast rules, but they still need to be recognisable. And never rely on a greyed-out button alone to explain why something can't be done. Tell people why.

07.7Dark mode, brands and white-labelling

Here's the payoff for all the naming work. A theme is simply a complete list of which paint each job points to. Below is the same little interface, built from the same named jobs, shown with two different lists.

Dark mode isn't just "invert the colours"

  • Avoid pure black. A very dark grey (Ledgerly uses grey 950) is easier on the eyes and leaves room to show depth.
  • Show depth with lighter surfaces. Shadows barely show on dark backgrounds, so raised cards and menus get slightly lighter instead.
  • Lighten the accent colours. Ledgerly's main action colour moves from brand 700 to brand 400 in dark mode, with dark text on top (5.96:1).
  • Check everything again. A pairing that passes in light mode can fail in dark mode, and the other way round.

IBM's Carbon, for example, offers four themes (two light, two dark) from a single set of colour jobs.[[c:carbon-themes]] [[doc]]

Several brands, or customers' brands

SituationWhat changesHow to set it up

Several brands you ownThe brand paints, sometimes fonts and corner roundingA brand level that swaps the brand paints, then light and dark per brand
White-label for customersA few things the customer chooses, like one colour and a logoGenerate the customer's ramp from their colour; let them change only those inputs

[[prac]] White-labelling safely

Let the customer pick one brand colour, then generate their ramp using your lightness curve, and point your jobs at it. Because lightness is fixed per shade, their "brand 700" ends up exactly as light as yours, so readability survives a colour choice you didn't make. Run the pairing checks automatically and fall back to a darker shade if a colour falls short (as green does at 600).

07.8Questions teams always ask about colour

How many colours should a design system have?

Few colour families, plenty of shades, and fewer named jobs than you'd expect.

Most products need one brand colour, one grey family and three or four status colours: five to seven ramps. At 11 shades each, that's 55 to 77 paints, but only 30 to 60 named jobs, and the jobs are what people actually choose from. [[prac]]

How many shades should each ramp have?

Between 10 and 13 is the normal range.

Tailwind uses 11, Radix 12 and Material 13.[[c:tailwind-v4,radix-scale,m3-color]] [[doc]] Fewer than about 9 leaves gaps for hover states and dark mode. More than about 13 gives you shades nobody can tell apart.

Is there a maximum?

No official one. The practical limit is how many your team can tell apart and remember.

The warning sign isn't the size of the palette. It's having several job names that mean almost the same thing: "text-muted", "text-subtle", "text-secondary", "text-tertiary". If you can't explain the difference between two names in one sentence, merge them.

How do we stop new colours creeping in?

Components use job names only, and an automatic check flags any colour code typed directly into product code. New job names need a reason ("which job doesn't exist yet?") and the owner's approval. And recount the "different colours in our code" number from Chapter 03 every quarter. It should go down.

How should we do gradients?

Blend in OKLCH, and name the start and end colours.

Blending in the traditional way passes through a dull, greyish middle. Blending in OKLCH keeps the middle bright and even. Modern CSS lets you choose which way to blend.

blended the traditional way (sRGB): blue 600 → amber 400
blended in OKLCH: blue 600 → amber 400
What about see-through colours and overlays?

Fine for overlays and hover layers; never for text.

The dark, see-through layer behind a pop-up dialog is a good use of transparency. Transparent text isn't: its readability changes depending on whatever is behind it, so you can't check it once and trust it. Give text solid colours.

Colour checklist

  • Palette built in OKLCH from one lightness curve
  • Brand colour used as a starting point; buttons use a readable shade
  • 5–7 colour families, 10–13 shades each, same numbering everywhere
  • Colour codes generated by a tool, not hand-picked
  • A deliberately tinted (or deliberately neutral) grey family
  • Simple pairing rules shared with designers
  • Raw colours and job names kept separate; components use job names only
  • A naming pattern written down
  • Hover, press, focus and disabled colours for every interactive role
  • Light and dark mode as two pointer lists
  • Every pairing checked automatically
  • Status colours always paired with an icon or words

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Use OKLCH: its lightness matches what eyes see, so palettes become predictable.
  2. Build each colour into a ramp where each shade number has the same lightness in every colour.
  3. Check readability with the WCAG ratios: 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for large text and UI edges.
  4. Name colours by their job and let components use only job names. Dark mode and new brands then become a switch.
