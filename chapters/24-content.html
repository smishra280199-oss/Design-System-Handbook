Chapter 24

Content and language in the system

A product can have perfectly consistent buttons and still feel chaotic if one says "Remove", another "Delete" and a third "Discard" for the same action. Words are part of the interface, so they belong in the design system too.

Read this if

Your product's wording feels inconsistent, you're about to translate your product, or writers and designers keep debating the same phrases.

What you'll leave with
  1. How to define a voice and adjust tone for different moments.
  2. A shared vocabulary and reusable writing formulas.
  3. What it takes to write for everyone, and to prepare for translation.

24.1Why words belong in the system

People read interfaces in fragments: a button label, a heading, an error message. When those fragments use different words for the same thing, people hesitate. Is "Archive" the same as "Remove"? Will "Discard" lose my work? Is a "workspace" the same as an "organisation"? Every hesitation is a small cost, and in a large product they add up.

ScenarioFive words for "make it go away"
The situation

Across Ledgerly, removing something uses "Delete", "Remove", "Archive", "Discard" and "Clear", chosen by whoever built each screen.

What goes wrong

Customers can't tell which actions are permanent. Support tickets ask how to recover "archived" invoices that were actually deleted. Translators get five different words to translate.

What the system adds

A vocabulary entry: "Delete = permanent, always confirmed. Archive = hidden, recoverable. Remove = taken out of a list, not deleted." Three words, three meanings, used everywhere.

Content in a design system usually covers four things: voice and tone (how the product sounds), vocabulary (which words mean what), formulas (reusable shapes for common messages) and localisation rules (what has to work in other languages). None of it needs to be long. A few pages, well maintained, do more than a 40-page style guide nobody opens.

24.2Voice and tone

Think of the difference between personality and mood. Your voice is your product's personality: it stays the same everywhere. Your tone is the mood: it adapts to the moment, the way a good colleague speaks differently when celebrating a win and when delivering bad news.

Ledgerly's voice, for example, might be: clear, calm and helpful. Its tone then adjusts:

MomentToneExample
SuccessBrief and warm. Don't over-celebrate routine tasks."Invoice sent to Acme Ltd."
ErrorCalm and specific. Never blame the person."We couldn't process this payment. The bank declined it. Try another card."
WarningClear about the consequence, and what to do."This invoice is overdue by 14 days. Send a reminder?"
Destructive actionSerious and explicit. Name exactly what will happen."Delete this customer? Their 12 invoices will also be deleted. This can't be undone."
First useEncouraging and practical. Point to the next step."No invoices yet. Create your first one to get paid faster."

A mistake we see often

Jokes in error messages

"Oops! Something went wonky!" might feel friendly in a design review. To someone whose payroll just failed, it feels dismissive. Keep personality for calm moments; in stressful moments, be clear and kind.

24.3A shared vocabulary

The single most useful content artefact is a simple vocabulary list: the words your product uses, what they mean, and the words to avoid. It helps designers, engineers, support and translators all at once.

UseMeaningDon't use
DeletePermanently remove. Always confirmed.Destroy, erase, discard
ArchiveHide from the main list; can be restored.Hide, store
CustomerA business you invoice.Client, account, buyer
WorkspaceYour company's area in Ledgerly, shared by its team.Organisation, tenant, account
Sign in / Sign outStart or end a session.Log in, login, log out
Due dateThe date an invoice must be paid by.Deadline, payment date

Small rules that add up

A handful of formatting rules prevent a surprising amount of inconsistency. Decide them once: sentence case or title case for headings and buttons (we recommend sentence case: "Send invoice", not "Send Invoice"); how dates are written; whether numbers use words or digits; how currencies are shown; and whether you write "you" and "your" when addressing people (we recommend it). Put them on one page, next to the vocabulary.

24.4Formulas for common messages

Most interface text falls into a few types of message. Giving each type a simple formula means anyone can write a good message, quickly, in the product's voice. Chapter 14 introduced the idea for individual components; here are the formulas side by side.

MessageFormulaExample
ButtonVerb + object"Send invoice", "Add customer"
ErrorWhat happened + why (if known) + what to do"Payment failed. The card has expired. Update the card details."
Field errorWhat to enter, specifically"Enter an amount between ₹1 and ₹10,00,000"
Confirmation dialogQuestion naming the action + consequence; buttons repeat the action"Delete 3 invoices? This can't be undone." [Delete invoices] [Cancel]
Empty stateWhat will appear here + how to start"Your payments will appear here. Connect a bank account to get started."
SuccessWhat happened, briefly"Customer added."

Notice the confirmation dialog's buttons. "Delete invoices" and "Cancel" are far clearer than "Yes" and "No", because people often read only the button. This one small habit prevents a lot of accidental deletions.

24.5Writing for everyone

Good interface writing is accessible writing. Plain words, short sentences and clear structure help everyone, and they matter most for people reading in a second language, people with cognitive or learning disabilities, and anyone who is tired, stressed or in a hurry.

PrincipleIn practice
Plain words"Use" not "utilise"; "start" not "initiate"; explain jargon or avoid it.
Front-load meaningPut the important words first: "Invoice sent", not "We have successfully sent your invoice".
Descriptive links"View payment history", not "Click here". Screen-reader users often navigate by a list of links.
Meaningful image textDescribe what an image tells people; mark purely decorative images so screen readers skip them.
Inclusive languageAvoid assumptions about gender, ability or culture; use "they" when you don't know; avoid idioms that don't translate.
Don't rely on direction or colour"Select the highlighted option" or "click the button on the right" fails for many people. Name the thing.

24.6Getting ready for other languages

Even if you only support one language today, a few habits now will save a painful rebuild later. Translation touches components, patterns and code, so it's very much a design system concern.

HabitWhy it matters
Leave room for longer textMany languages need noticeably more space than English for the same message. Components should wrap or grow, not cut text off.
Support right-to-leftArabic and Hebrew read right to left; layouts and directional icons should mirror. Logical CSS properties make this mostly automatic.[[c:mdn-logical]]
Never build sentences from pieces"You have " + count + " invoices" breaks in languages with different word order or plural rules. Keep whole sentences together, with placeholders.
Handle plurals properlySome languages have several plural forms, not just "one" and "many". Use your platform's built-in plural rules.[[c:mdn-intl]]
Format dates, numbers and money by locale"1,00,000" in India is "100,000" elsewhere; dates differ too. Use built-in formatting rather than hand-written formats.[[c:mdn-intl]]
Give translators context"Post" could be a verb or a noun. Add a note or a screenshot to every string.

Who owns the words?

In larger companies, content designers or UX writers own the voice, vocabulary and formulas, just as system designers own the visual foundations. In smaller ones, it's often a senior designer or product manager with a little protected time. Either way, treat content guidance like any other part of the system: it has an owner, it's versioned, changes are announced, and teams can propose improvements. Some teams even add simple automatic checks, flagging words from the "don't use" list in code reviews.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Words are part of the interface; inconsistent words confuse people as much as inconsistent buttons.
  2. Keep one voice, adjust the tone to the moment, and be calm and specific when things go wrong.
  3. A short vocabulary list and a few message formulas do more than a long style guide.
  4. Write plainly and inclusively, and build components and code ready for other languages from the start.
