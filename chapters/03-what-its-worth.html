Chapter 03

What a design system is worth

"It'll make us more consistent and efficient" won't get a budget approved. This chapter explains where the value really comes from, what has actually been measured, and how to measure it in your own company.

Read this if

You need to convince a CFO, a board or a sceptical engineering leader, or prove that your current system is earning its keep.

What you'll leave with
  1. The three ways a design system creates value, in plain terms.
  2. An honest view of the published evidence, including where it's weak.
  3. A short list of numbers you can start tracking this quarter.

03.1Three ways a design system saves you money

Every real benefit of a design system comes back to one of three simple ideas. If someone promises you a benefit that doesn't fit one of these, be a little suspicious.

01

Decide once

Teams stop re-deciding the same things: which grey, how much padding, how errors should look. That time goes to the problems that are actually new.

02

Build once

A component is built, tested and made accessible once, then reused everywhere. A bug fixed in it is fixed everywhere.

03

Change once

Because products point to shared parts instead of copies, changing the source changes every product. Rebrands, themes and accessibility fixes become updates, not projects.

Here's an everyday example of each.

Decide once. A designer starts a new settings page. Without a system, she spends the morning choosing toggle styles, spacing and section headings, then an hour in review defending those choices. With a system, those choices already exist. She spends the morning on what's actually hard: how to explain a confusing billing option to customers.

Build once. An engineer finds that dropdown menus can't be closed with the Escape key. Without a system, that bug exists in each team's own dropdown, so it gets fixed (or not) five separate times. With a system, it's fixed once and every team gets the fix with their next update.

Change once. Marketing refreshes the brand colour. Without a system, it's a project touching every product. With a system, it's one change and a release.

The first two save time steadily, a little every day. The third saves time in big lumps, whenever something changes, and over a product's life it's often the biggest saving of all. That's worth remembering when you build your business case: if you only count the first two, you're underselling the system.

03.2What's actually been measured (and what hasn't)

Let's be straight with you: the published research on design system value is thinner than the marketing suggests. Many of the impressive percentages you'll see online trace back to a handful of small studies, several run by companies that sell design tools. That doesn't mean the benefits aren't real. It means you should trust the mechanisms above, and measure the results in your own company rather than borrowing someone else's number.

Here are the three numbers worth knowing, along with how far to trust each one.

34%faster to complete a design task when designers had a relevant, up-to-date design system.[[doc]] Figma's internal experiment.[[c:figma-value]] Run by a tool vendor, with limited detail on the sample.
47%faster to code a simple form using IBM's Carbon system than from scratch (2 hours vs 4.2).[[doc]] Sparkbox.[[c:sparkbox-carbon]] Only 8 developers, and they always built from scratch first.
79.1%of the world's top million home pages had text that was too low in contrast to read comfortably.[[doc]] WebAIM Million 2025.[[c:webaim-2025]] A problem a design system can fix at the source.

Why mention the weaknesses? Because the first engineer who reads the Sparkbox study will notice that the developers built the form from scratch first, so by the second attempt they already understood the design. If your business case leans on that 47%, it falls over at the first question. If it leans on the mechanism ("we'll stop building dropdowns five times") plus your own measurements, it holds.

Two more findings help explain why consistency is a business issue, not a cosmetic one. A 1995 study by Kurosu and Kashimura found that people rated better-looking interfaces as easier to use, even when they weren't.[[c:kurosu]] And a 2003 Stanford study with more than 2,500 participants found that a website's overall look was the factor people mentioned most when judging whether they could trust it.[[c:fogg]] [[doc]] Neither study is about design systems specifically. But they show that a product which looks stitched together pays a real price in trust, and a design system is one of the most reliable ways to avoid looking stitched together.

A mistake we see often

Borrowing someone else's percentage

"Design systems make teams 47% faster" sounds great in a slide deck and collapses under one good question. Use published studies to show the idea is sound, then build your case on your own "before" numbers (see 03.4). Chapter 04 walks through a cautious way to do this.

03.3Nine outcomes, and why each one happens

When people say a design system "improves quality" or "speeds up delivery", it helps to know why, so you can explain it and later prove it. Here's each outcome with the reason behind it and something you can actually measure.

Accessibility deserves its own line in the budget

Accessibility is where the business case has changed fastest in recent years. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 set out testable rules for things like text contrast, visible keyboard focus and the size of tap targets.[[c:wcag22]] [[std]] More and more regulation points to these rules. The European Accessibility Act, for example, applies to many digital products and services sold in the EU from 28 June 2025.[[c:eu-eaa]]

A design system is the cheapest place to meet these rules. When the colour your whole product uses for body text is checked for contrast once, every screen passes. When the system's dialog handles keyboard focus properly, every dialog in every product does. Fixing accessibility screen by screen fixes it once, and only until the next screen gets built.

03.4Numbers you can start tracking this quarter

Layer One has a rule about this:

Measure time saved, not usage.Rule #8 — Layer One

Here's what we mean. It's tempting to report "our button has been used 12,000 times!" That tells you the system is being used. It doesn't tell you it's worth it. Track usage, by all means, because it's useful for the system team, but report outcomes to leadership.

You don't need fancy tooling to start. Most of these can be pulled with a few scripts and your existing project tracker.

[[prac]] The one thing to do first

Count before you build

Count your duplicates, hard-coded colours and accessibility errors before any system work ships. Without a starting point, every later number is just an opinion. With one, a single slide that says "different colours in our code: 412 → 61" does more for next year's budget than any industry statistic.

03.5When the value shows up, and when it doesn't

Design systems are a bit like planting a tree. There's a cost up front, the first year looks unimpressive, and the real payoff comes later. Knowing the shape of that curve helps you set honest expectations with leadership.

Four ways a design system can lose money

It's only fair to say that a badly run system can cost more than it saves. These are the four patterns we see most:

  • Nobody uses it. A system that only 10% of the product uses has nearly all the cost and almost none of the benefit. Adoption multiplies every outcome above.
  • It tries to do everything. A button with forty options is slower to use than just building the thing yourself.
  • It's a bottleneck. If every request waits weeks for the system team, product teams will work around it, and honestly, they'll be right to.
  • It stops changing. A system that doesn't keep up with the product becomes something teams fight against instead of something that helps them.

All four are problems of how the system is run, not of what's in it. That's what Chapter 05 is about.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Value comes from three things: deciding once, building once and changing once. The third is often the biggest.
  2. The published evidence is real but thin. Use it to show the idea works, not as your headline number.
  3. Count your "before" numbers now: duplicates, hard-coded values, accessibility errors.
  4. Report time and money saved to leadership, not how many times a button was used.
