Chapter 03

What a design system is worth

"Consistency and efficiency" is not a business case. This chapter separates what has actually been measured from what is merely claimed, and gives you a way to measure your own outcomes.

Read this if

You need to justify investment to a CFO, a board or a sceptical engineering leader, or you need to prove that an existing system is working.

What you'll leave with
  1. An honest picture of the published evidence, including its limits.
  2. The mechanism behind each of nine business outcomes, so you can argue causally rather than by anecdote.
  3. A KPI framework with formulas you can baseline this quarter.

03.1Where the value actually comes from

Every business benefit of a design system traces back to three mechanisms. If a claimed benefit can't be traced to one of them, be suspicious of it.

Mechanism 1

Decide once

Teams stop re-making the same decisions: which grey, how much padding, how errors behave. Time moves from re-deciding to the problems that are actually new.

Mechanism 2

Build once

Components are built, tested and made accessible once, then reused. Defects are fixed once and the fix ships everywhere.

Mechanism 3

Change once

Because products reference tokens and components rather than copies, a decision changed at the source propagates: rebrands, themes, accessibility fixes.

The first two produce savings that accrue steadily with usage. The third produces savings in lumps, at the moments of change, and it is often the larger of the three over a product's lifetime. Business cases that only count the first two undersell the system; business cases that promise the third without a token architecture overpromise it.

03.2The evidence, honestly

The public evidence base for design system ROI is thinner than the marketing around it suggests. Many percentages in circulation trace back to a small number of studies, several of them run by vendors. That doesn't mean the benefits aren't real. It means you should rely on the mechanisms and measure your own outcomes, rather than borrowing someone else's number.

Common mistake

Putting someone else's percentage in your business case

"Design systems make teams 47% faster" will be challenged by the first engineer who reads the study, and your credibility goes with it. Use published findings to show the mechanism is real, then build your case on your own baseline using the KPIs in 03.4. Chapter 04 shows how to do this with conservative assumptions.

Accessibility deserves its own line in the budget

Accessibility is the outcome where the business case has moved fastest. WCAG 2.2 sets testable success criteria for contrast, focus visibility and target size.[[c:wcag22]] [[std]] Regulation increasingly references these criteria: the European Accessibility Act, for example, applies to many digital products and services placed on the EU market from 28 June 2025.[[c:eu-eaa]] A design system is the most efficient place to meet them, because a contrast-checked colour token or a component with correct focus behaviour fixes the issue in every product that uses it. Remediating screen by screen fixes it once, and only until the next screen is built.

03.4A KPI framework you can baseline this quarter

Measure time saved, not usage.Rule #8 — Layer One

Usage metrics (component import counts, Figma insertions) tell you whether the system is used. They don't tell you whether it is worth it. Track both, but report outcomes to leadership.

[[prac]] Baselining tip

Measure before you build

Run the duplication, token-coverage and a11y counts during the audit, before any system work ships. Without a baseline, every later number is an opinion. With one, a single slide ("unique colour values: 412 → 61") does more for continued funding than any industry statistic.

03.5When value shows up, and when it doesn't

When a design system destroys value

  • Nobody adopts it. A system with 10% adoption has all of the cost and almost none of the benefit. Adoption is the multiplier on every outcome above.
  • It is too abstract. Components with 40 props that try to serve every case are slower to use than writing the UI by hand.
  • It is a bottleneck. If every new need waits weeks for the system team, product teams will route around it, and they will be right to.
  • It is frozen. A system that doesn't evolve with the product becomes a constraint teams fight rather than a tool they use.

Chapter 05 is about the operating model that prevents all four.
