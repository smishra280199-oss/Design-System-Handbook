Chapter 36

Governance, decision-making and ownership

Who has the final say on a token change, a new pattern or an accessibility standard? What happens when design and engineering disagree, or two business units want opposite things? Governance answers these questions before they become arguments. This chapter shows how to keep it light in small companies and clear in large ones.

Read this if

Decisions about the system stall or get re-opened, people aren't sure who decides, or you need governance that scales without becoming bureaucracy.

What you'll leave with
  1. A decision-rights matrix for every kind of system decision.
  2. Decision processes matched to impact, plus an escalation path.
  3. A decision log, approval gates, and how governance should differ by company size.

36.1What governance is, and isn't

New wordGovernanceThe agreed way decisions about the system are made: who decides what, how, how fast, and how it's recorded.

Governance has a bad reputation, because people picture committees and approval forms. Good design system governance is the opposite: it's what lets most decisions happen quickly, by the right person, without a meeting. It only slows things down for the few decisions that genuinely affect everyone.

ScenarioThree months to change a border radius
The situation

Brand wants rounder corners. The system designer agrees. An engineering lead worries about layouts. A product director wants to wait until after a launch.

What goes wrong

Nobody knows who decides. The topic comes up in five meetings over three months. Everyone is frustrated; nothing happens.

What governance changes

Token changes are decided by the system lead after a one-week comment period. The engineering concern becomes a release plan; the launch concern sets the date. Decided in ten days, logged with reasons.

Brad Frost's design system governance process, which many teams use as a starting point, makes the same point in a different way: most work should flow through a clear, predictable path from "use the system" to "propose a change" to "release", with the design system team and product teams each knowing their part.[[c:frost-governance]] Governance is that path, plus clarity about who decides at each step.

36.2Decision rights: who has the final say

Here's a decision-rights matrix for a hybrid system at the mid-sized or unicorn stage. "Decides" means accountable for the final call; "approves" means can block; "consulted" means must be asked before the decision. Adjust names to your organisation, but keep one decider per row. [[prac]]

Notice two patterns. Decisions are pushed down to the people closest to the work wherever possible, and leadership only decides things that are genuinely about direction or money. And accessibility has a real veto: an approver who can block a change that fails the standard. That's deliberate. Accessibility is the one area where "we'll fix it later" multiplies harm across every product.

36.3Matching the process to the decision

Not every decision needs the same process. A useful question is: how many people does this affect, and how easy is it to undo? Small, reversible decisions should be fast; big, hard-to-undo decisions deserve more care.

Decision typeExamplesProcessTime to decide
EverydayBug fixes, docs, small enhancements, new variantsThe owner decides, in reviewDays
SignificantNew components, token value changes, new patterns, deprecationsShort written proposal; one-week comment period; decider decides1–2 weeks
FoundationalNew framework, breaking API changes across the library, new brand, accessibility standardProposal; wider comment period; leadership or council review2–6 weeks
ExceptionBreaking a rule for one productShort request; named approver; expiry date; loggedDays

The comment period matters as much as the decision. People accept decisions they disagree with far more readily when they had a genuine chance to be heard, and when the reasons are written down. That's also why Layer One's sixth rule applies so strongly here: every decision comes with a reason.

36.4When people disagree: the escalation path

Disagreement is healthy; endless disagreement isn't. An escalation path says where a stuck decision goes next, and how long each step can take. Publish it, so nobody needs to invent one in the heat of an argument.

  1. The owner decides

    The person with decision rights (36.2) makes the call after hearing the concerns. Most disagreements end here.

  2. System lead

    If someone believes the decision is wrong for the system as a whole, the system lead reviews it within a week.

  3. Design and engineering leads together

    For conflicts between design and engineering, or between product teams, the relevant leads decide jointly within two weeks.

  4. Council or leadership

    Only for decisions affecting several business units or company direction. Decided at the next session.

[[prac]] Disagree and commit

Once a decision is made through the agreed process, everyone commits to it, including those who argued against it. They can propose revisiting it later with new evidence, through the same process. Without this norm, every decision is reopened in the next meeting.

36.5Standards and approval gates

Some things shouldn't be decided case by case. They're standards, applied as gates that every change passes through automatically or in review.

Gates are where governance and automation meet (Chapter 26). The more gates are checked automatically, the less governance needs people's time, and the more consistently standards are applied.

36.6Accountability: the decision log and ownership registry

Two simple records make governance trustworthy.

DECISION LOG ENTRY portal → decisions

DS-042 · Increase default button height from 36px to 40px · Decided 2 October by the system lead

Context

Research showed buttons were hard to hit on touch laptops; 36px is below our comfortable-target guideline.

Options considered

(a) Patch release now · (b) New "large" default only for new work · (c) Breaking change in 5.0 with notice

Decision

(c), shipping in 5.0 on 18 November, with visual diffs and a layout checklist.

Consulted

Champions (all teams), engineering leads, accessibility specialist. Concern about table rows addressed with a compact variant.

Revisit if

More than two teams report blocking layout problems during the beta.

The decision log stops decisions being re-litigated, because the reasons are written down, and gives newcomers the history behind the system. The ownership registry is a simple page listing every component, pattern and token group with its owner, so everyone knows who decides and who to ask. Both live on the portal (Chapter 32).

A mistake we see often

A council for everything

Large organisations sometimes route every decision through a governance council. Councils meet monthly, so everything takes a month. Keep the council for the handful of decisions that really cross business units, and let the decision-rights matrix handle the rest.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Good governance makes most decisions fast; it only slows down the few that affect everyone.
  2. Write down one decider for each kind of decision, and give accessibility a real veto.
  3. Match the process to the impact, publish an escalation path, and expect everyone to disagree and commit.
  4. Automate standards as gates, keep a decision log and an ownership registry, and grow governance only as you grow.
