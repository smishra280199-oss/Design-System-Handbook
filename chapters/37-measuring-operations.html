Chapter 37

Measuring how well the system runs

Chapter 03 asked whether a design system is worth it. This chapter asks a different question: is the way we run it working? A team can be busy every day and still not be helping. The right measures tell you where the operating model is strong, where it's failing, and what to change.

Read this if

You need to know whether your processes, team and rhythms are effective, or you report on the system to leadership.

What you'll leave with
  1. A catalogue of operational metrics, with how to measure each one.
  2. Which metrics matter at each stage of maturity, and a one-page dashboard.
  3. How to turn metrics into improvements, report them, and avoid the common traps.

37.1Busy is not the same as effective

ScenarioForty components shipped, adoption flat
The situation

A system team proudly reports 40 new components in a year, 26 releases and 12 workshops.

What's missing

Adoption hasn't moved. Requests wait two months. Satisfaction has dropped. The team measured how much it did, not whether it helped.

What changes

The team starts tracking request turnaround, adoption by team and satisfaction. Within a quarter they see the real problem: slow triage and missing docs, not missing components.

It helps to separate three kinds of measure. Outputs are what the team produces: components, releases, workshops. Operational measures show how well the system runs: how fast requests are handled, how many people get help, how current the docs are. Outcomes are the results for the business: adoption, time saved, quality (Chapter 03). Outputs are easy to count and the least meaningful. This chapter focuses on the middle layer, which connects the team's work to the outcomes.

37.3What to measure at each stage

Measuring has a cost, and a small team that tracks twenty metrics will spend more time on spreadsheets than on the system. Start with a few, and add more as the system and the team grow. [[prac]]

Whatever the stage, keep one rule from Chapter 03: baseline before you change anything. A metric without a starting point can't show improvement.

37.4A one-page dashboard

Put the metrics you choose on one page, in four groups, and review it monthly. If it doesn't fit on one page, you're tracking too much.

37.5From metrics to improvements

Metrics are only useful if they change what you do. Each warning sign usually points to a specific part of the operating model. Here's how we read the most common ones.

What you seeLikely causeWhat to try
First-response time risingTriage capacity or skipped triage meetingsProtect weekly triage; add a support rota (Chapter 29)
Docs views low, but the same questions keep comingPeople can't find the docs, or don't trust themImprove search and synonyms; link answers to docs (Chapter 32)
Adoption lagging in one teamMissing onboarding, a real blocker, or no championInterview the team; assign a champion (Chapters 30, 31)

Review the dashboard monthly with the team, pick one or two things to improve, and check next month whether they moved. Small, steady improvements to the operating model compound just like improvements to the product.

37.6Reporting to leadership

Leaders don't need the whole dashboard. They need to know whether the investment is working, what's in the way, and what you need from them. Once a quarter, one page.

QUARTERLY REPORT to design and engineering leadership

Design system · Q3 summary

Headline

Adoption rose from 64% to 68%. The new filter pattern replaced four local versions and cut filter-related UI bugs by half in two products.

Value delivered

Dark mode shipped to all products in six weeks using the token architecture, against an earlier estimate of two quarters. Accessibility issues in components down from 11 to 4.

How we're running

Median request response: 2 days. 6 contributions from 4 teams. Satisfaction up 9 points to 71%.

Risks

Reports team adoption at 41%; their roadmap conflicts with migration. Two system engineers at capacity.

Asks

Agree migration time in Reports' Q4 plan. Approve one engineering hire for native components.

Notice the order: outcomes first, then how the operation is running, then risks and specific asks. Leaders who see a clear link from the system to results they care about keep funding it. The financial model in Chapter 04 shows how to put numbers on "value delivered" when you need them.

37.7Traps to avoid

Trap

Vanity metrics

"12,000 button instances!" sounds impressive and says nothing about value. Prefer measures linked to time, quality and satisfaction (Rule #8).

Trap

Gaming the number

If adoption becomes a target, teams may wrap old components in system ones to inflate it. Pair every number with a conversation.

Trap

Blaming teams

Low adoption in a team is information about the system, not a failing of the team. Use metrics to find where to help, never to rank teams publicly.

Trap

Measuring everything

Twenty metrics nobody reviews are worse than four that drive a monthly decision. Start small, and drop metrics that never change anything.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Measure how well the system runs, not just how much the team produces.
  2. Choose a few metrics for your stage, baseline them, and keep them on a one-page dashboard.
  3. Read each warning sign as a pointer to a specific part of the operating model, and improve one thing at a time.
  4. Report outcomes, operations, risks and asks to leadership quarterly, and avoid vanity, gaming and blame.
