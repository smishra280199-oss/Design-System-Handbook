Chapter 04

The economics of design systems

The biggest misconception about design systems is that they need a large upfront investment. They don't. Investment is a dial, not a switch. This chapter shows how to set the dial and how to know when to turn it up.

Read this if

You need a budget number, a staffing plan or an ROI model, or someone has quoted you a seven-figure estimate and you want to understand what drives it.

What you'll leave with
  1. The ten factors that drive cost, so you can scope rather than guess.
  2. Three budget scenarios built from roles and FTE-months, with every assumption stated.
  3. An ROI model you can run with your own numbers, plus an honest view of when it breaks even.

04.1How to read the numbers in this chapter

Salaries differ by a factor of five or more between markets, so a dollar figure from one market is meaningless in another. This chapter therefore budgets in FTE-months (one full-time person for one month) and converts to currency only at the end, using three example rates you should replace with your own.

[[ill]] All figures in this chapter are illustrative

They are built from stated assumptions, not measured from real projects. The point is the method: change the assumptions to match your organisation and the model recalculates. The full model is in scripts/generate-data.mjs in the playbook's repository.

04.2What determines the investment

Ten factors explain most of the difference between a system that costs a few FTE-months and one that costs a hundred.

Cost driverLower cost when…Higher cost when…Impact
PlatformsWeb onlyWeb + iOS + Android (+ desktop): each needs native componentsVery high
FrameworksOne front-end frameworkReact and Angular and a legacy stack in parallelVery high
Brands and themesOne brand, light modeDark mode, multiple brands, white-label for customersHigh

Accessibility targetWCAG 2.2 AA built in from the startRetrofitting AA (or AAA) onto existing componentsMedium
Documentation depthUsage guidance and code examplesFull public docs site, content guidelines, tutorialsMedium
Team distributionCo-located, one time zoneMany teams, vendors, time zonesMedium
Tooling and automationManual token export, simple packageToken pipeline, visual regression tests, linting, Figma–code syncMedium (pays back)
Governance overheadOne product team as ownerCouncils, multiple business units, compliance sign-offMedium

Platforms and frameworks dominate. Every additional platform roughly means another implementation of every component. That's why token architecture comes first: tokens are the one layer that is written once and compiled to every platform.

Common mistake

Staffing a design system with designers only

It produces an excellent Figma library and no adoption, because adoption happens in code. Our rule of thumb is at least as much engineering capacity as design capacity on the core team, and more once you ship multiple platforms. [[prac]]

04.5Three budget scenarios

Each scenario lists year-one effort by role, then converts the total at three example loaded monthly costs per person (salary plus overheads): $6,000, $12,000 and $20,000. These are example inputs, not market data; substitute your own. [[ill]]

Reading the scenarios

  • The startup system costs less than one engineer for a year. It buys tokens, a themed off-the-shelf library and a handful of custom components: enough to stay coherent through Series A.
  • The mid-sized system is a small team, not a department. Two to three people are enough if the scope is one platform plus mobile web and product teams contribute.
  • Enterprise cost is driven by platforms and brands. Engineering is over half of the enterprise effort here, and native iOS and Android work alone is more than a fifth of it.

For comparison, zeroheight's 2025 survey reports average dedicated team sizes of about three people at companies under 100 employees, four at 100–499, and nine at 500+.[[c:zeroheight-2025]] [[doc]] Teams rarely grow beyond 20–25 people, even in very large organisations, because at that scale contribution from product teams does the scaling.

04.7Ongoing maintenance costs

The most expensive mistake in design system budgeting is funding the build and not the run. A system without maintenance degrades within a year: components lag behind product needs, teams fork them, and adoption reverses.

What "run" includes

  • Bug fixes and accessibility fixes in components
  • New variants and components as products evolve
  • Dependency and framework upgrades (e.g. a React major version)
  • Design tool library maintenance and releases
  • Documentation, office hours, onboarding
  • Reviewing and merging contributions
  • Measuring adoption and health

[[prac]] Budget run as a team, not a percentage

Rather than "maintenance = X% of build", plan for the team that built the system to stay, at a similar or slightly smaller size, with its time shifting from building to enabling. In our experience, systems whose builders are reassigned after launch rarely keep adoption.

04.8Calculating ROI

A defensible ROI model has three parts: hours saved by reuse, avoided costs at moments of change, and the cost of the team. The formulas:

// Annual value from reuse (efficiency)
hours_saved = people × focused_hours × UI_share × adoption × efficiency_gain
value       = hours_saved × loaded_hourly_rate

// Annual value from change-once events (count only those actually planned)
avoided     = Σ (cost_without_system − cost_with_system) for rebrands, themes, a11y fixes

// Return
ROI         = (value + avoided − system_cost) ÷ system_cost

A worked example: Ledgerly

Ledgerly, our fictional B2B finance product, has 40 engineers and 6 designers. We assume a 20% efficiency gain on work the system covers. That is deliberately well below the 34% and 47% figures from 03.2, which come from best-case tasks. Adoption ramps over three years. We count only efficiency savings, so the model leaves out every change-once event.

That result is deliberately unflattering, and it is the most useful number in this chapter. It shows that at 40 engineers, efficiency alone barely justifies a two-person dedicated team. The case rests on adoption speed and on change-once events, which is exactly where to focus the plan.

04.9Start small, invest progressively

Fund each stage from the evidence produced by the last one. Each step has a gate: a measurable condition that unlocks the next round of investment.

  1. Audit — 2 weeks

    Inventory styles, components and tokens in design and code; baseline duplication, token coverage and accessibility. Gate: leadership agrees on the ranked problem list.

  2. Foundations and tokens — 1–2 months, 1–2 people part-time

    Colour, type, spacing, layout and elevation tokens in code and design. Gate: one product team ships new work using only tokens.

  3. The ten most-used components — 2–3 months

    Pick by frequency in the audit, not by what's interesting. Gate: pilot team measurably faster or fewer UI defects.

  4. Docs, releases and contribution — ongoing

    Make it usable by teams who didn't build it. Gate: a second team adopts without hand-holding; first external contribution merged.

  5. Scale — themes, platforms, governance

    Only now invest in multi-brand, native platforms and formal governance. Gate: adoption above ~60% and a planned change-once event that needs it.

The economics in one sentence

Tokens are cheap and pay back immediately; components pay back with adoption; governance pays back with scale. Fund them in that order.
