Chapter 04

The economics of design systems

"Isn't a design system a huge investment?" It can be, but it doesn't have to be. Spending on a design system works more like a volume knob than an on/off switch. This chapter shows you how to set the knob, and when to turn it up.

Read this if

You need a budget, a staffing plan or a return-on-investment argument, or someone has quoted you a frightening number and you want to know where it came from.

What you'll leave with
  1. The handful of things that make a design system cheap or expensive.
  2. Three realistic budgets, with every assumption out in the open.
  3. A simple, honest way to work out whether it pays off for you.

04.1A quick word on the numbers

New wordFTE-monthOne person working full-time for one month. "6 FTE-months" could be one person for six months or three people for two.

Salaries vary enormously between countries and cities. A budget written in dollars for San Francisco means nothing in Bangalore, and the other way round. So in this chapter we count effort in FTE-months first, which works anywhere, and only then turn it into money using example monthly costs that you should swap for your own.

When we say "monthly cost per person", we mean the loaded cost: salary plus everything else a company pays for that person, such as benefits, equipment, office space and taxes. That's typically noticeably more than salary alone.

The numbers here come from clearly stated assumptions, not from measuring real projects. The method is the valuable part: change the assumptions to match your company and the answers change with them. The full model is in scripts/generate-data.mjs in this playbook's source files.

04.2What makes a design system cheap or expensive

When two companies get wildly different quotes for "a design system", the difference almost always comes down to the same few factors. The biggest two are platforms and technology: every extra platform (web, iPhone, Android) means building every component again, in a different language, by different engineers.

FactorCheaper when…More expensive when…Impact
PlatformsYou only build for the webYou build for web, iPhone and Android (and maybe desktop)Very high
TechnologyEveryone uses the same front-end frameworkSome teams use React, some Angular, some an old in-house stackVery high
Brands and themesOne brand, light mode onlyDark mode, several brands, or white-labelled versions for customersHigh
Existing messThe product is youngYears of inconsistent UI need to be moved overHigh
Number of components15–25 everyday components60+, including complex ones like data grids and chartsHigh
AccessibilityBuilt in from the startAdded afterwards to components that weren't designed for itMedium
DocumentationClear usage notes and code examplesA full public documentation site with tutorialsMedium
How spread out teams areOne office, one time zoneMany teams, outside agencies, many time zonesMedium
ToolingValues copied over by handAutomated pipelines, visual testing, automatic checksMedium (pays back)

This also explains why we always start with named values (tokens). They're the one part of the system that's written once and then automatically turned into code for every platform. A component has to be rebuilt per platform; a colour name doesn't.

04.3Who you need

A design system is built and run by a small mix of people. In a startup these might all be part-time hats worn by people with other jobs. In an enterprise they're full-time roles.

RoleWhat they actually doWhen you need it
System leadDecides what gets built next, keeps leadership on side, tracks whether teams are using it. Treats the system like a product.From Stage 2. Often half of a design or engineering lead's time.
System designerDesigns the foundations and components, keeps the Figma library tidy, writes usage guidance.From day one
System engineerBuilds components in code, sets up the pipeline that turns named values into code, publishes updates.From day one. This is the role most often missing.
Mobile engineersBuild the iPhone and Android versions of components from the same named values.When you have native apps
Accessibility specialistChecks components against the rules and tests with screen readers.Part-time from Stage 2
Writer / docsOrganises the documentation and writes guidance on wording and tone.Part-time from Stage 3
ContributorsDesigners and engineers in product teams who suggest and build improvements.From Stage 3, with time set aside in their team's plans

A mistake we see often

A design system team made only of designers

You end up with a beautiful Figma library and very little real-world use, because the product is built in code, not in Figma. As a rule of thumb, have at least as much engineering time on the core team as design time, and more once you support several platforms. [[prac]]

04.6A dedicated team, or your existing team?

This is one of the most common questions we get. The honest answer is "it depends on your size", so here's how to think about it.

Owned by an existing product teamA dedicated design system team
How it worksA designer and an engineer in a product team get protected time, often one or two days a week, to look after the system.A team whose whole job is the system, treating the product teams as its customers.
Good atCheap, and stays close to real product needs.Focus, quality, and serving many teams and platforms.
Watch out forDeadlines eat the protected time; the system quietly bends towards that one team's needs.Drifting away from real product work; becoming a bottleneck if others can't contribute.
Usually fitsOne product, fewer than about 30 engineers.Two or more products or platforms, roughly 40+ engineers.

For a reality check, zeroheight's 2025 survey found dedicated teams averaging about three people at companies under 100 employees, four at 100–499, and nine at 500 or more.[[c:zeroheight-2025]] [[doc]] Even in very large companies, teams rarely grow past 20 to 25 people. At that size, the extra capacity comes from product teams contributing rather than from a bigger central team.

04.7The cost nobody budgets for: keeping it running

The most expensive budgeting mistake isn't overspending on the build. It's spending nothing after launch. Picture a team that builds a great system, celebrates the launch, and then gets reassigned to "real" product work. Within a year the components are behind what the products need, teams quietly make their own copies, and people stop using the system. All that investment slowly leaks away.

What "keeping it running" involves

  • Fixing bugs and accessibility problems in components
  • Adding new versions of components as products change
  • Upgrading when the underlying technology moves on
  • Keeping the Figma library tidy and in step with the code
  • Documentation, office hours and onboarding new people
  • Reviewing and accepting contributions from other teams
  • Checking whether teams are actually using it

[[prac]] Plan for a team, not a percentage

People often ask, "what percentage of the build cost is maintenance?" We think that's the wrong question. Plan for the people who built the system to stay with it, at a similar or slightly smaller size, with their time shifting from building new things to helping teams use what exists. In our experience, systems whose builders move on right after launch rarely stay in use.

04.8Does it pay off? A simple way to work it out

Return on investment (ROI) sounds like finance jargon, but the idea is simple: is the time and money the system saves bigger than what it costs? There are three pieces to add up.

  1. Time saved from reuse

    How many people build UI, how much of their time goes on UI, how much of that work the system covers, and how much faster it makes them. Multiply those together to get hours, then multiply by an hourly cost.

  2. Big changes made cheaper

    For each planned change, like a rebrand, dark mode or an accessibility clean-up, estimate the cost with and without a system. Only count changes that are actually on the roadmap.

  3. What the system costs

    The system team's cost for the year.

// The same thing, written as a formula
hours_saved = people × working_hours × share_of_time_on_UI × adoption × speed_up
value       = hours_saved × hourly_cost + savings_from_planned_big_changes
ROI         = (value − system_cost) ÷ system_cost

A worked example: Ledgerly

Ledgerly is the fictional finance product we use throughout this book. It has 40 engineers and 6 designers. We'll assume the system makes work it covers 20% faster. That's deliberately well below the 34% and 47% from Chapter 03, because those came from best-case tasks. We'll let adoption grow over three years, and we'll count only time saved from reuse, ignoring every big change, to see the most cautious picture.

We'll admit that's not the flashy answer people expect, and we think that's exactly why it's useful. It shows that at 40 engineers, a two-person team doesn't pay for itself on speed alone in year one. The case depends on two things: getting teams to adopt the system quickly, and the big changes that are coming. That tells you exactly where to focus.

04.9Start small, and earn the next step

You don't have to commit to the whole thing up front. The approach we recommend is to fund the system one step at a time, and let each step earn the next. Each step has a clear "gate": something you can point to that justifies the next round of investment.

  1. Audit — two weeks

    Find out what you have in design and code, and count your duplicates, hard-coded values and accessibility problems. Gate: leadership agrees on the top problems.

  2. Foundations and named values — one to two months, part-time

    Colours, text, spacing and layout, named and shared by design and code. Gate: one product team ships new work using only the named values.

  3. The ten most-used components — two to three months

    Pick them by how often they appear in your audit, not by which ones are fun to design. Gate: the pilot team is measurably faster, or has fewer UI bugs.

  4. Documentation, releases and contribution — ongoing

    Make it usable by people who didn't build it. Gate: a second team adopts it without hand-holding, and someone outside the core team contributes something.

  5. Scale up — themes, platforms, governance

    Only now invest in multiple brands, native platforms and formal decision-making. Gate: most new work uses the system, and there's a real upcoming change that needs it.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Cost is driven mostly by platforms, technology and brands. Named values are the cheapest part and pay back first.
  2. Staff the system with engineers as well as designers, and keep the team after launch.
  3. Be honest in your ROI: on speed alone, payback takes time; adoption and big upcoming changes make the case.
  4. Fund it in steps, and let each step earn the next.
