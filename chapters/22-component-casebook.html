Chapter 22

A casebook of real situations

Principles are easy to agree with and hard to apply on a Tuesday afternoon with a deadline. This chapter works through twelve situations design system teams meet again and again, showing the options, what we'd recommend and why.

Read this if

You want to see how the ideas in Parts 03 and 04 play out in practice, or you're facing one of these situations right now.

What you'll leave with
  1. Worked answers to twelve common component situations.
  2. A repeatable way of thinking: options, recommendation, reasons.
  3. A migration plan you can adapt for retiring old components.

22.1When teams need different things

Each case follows the same shape: the situation, the realistic options, what we'd recommend, and why. They're composites of situations we see in practice, not specific clients. [[ill]]

Case 1Two teams, two very different date pickers

Payments needs a picker that shows bank holidays and blocks weekends. Reporting needs to select ranges of whole months. Both ask for "the date picker".

Options

(a) One picker with options for everything. (b) Two separate pickers. (c) A solid core picker, plus extensions built on it.

Recommendation

(c). The core picker handles single dates and ranges, with a way to mark days as unavailable. Reporting builds a month-range picker as a shared extension; payments supplies its holiday list to the core picker.

Why

Option (a) creates a level 4 monster. Option (b) duplicates the hardest part, the calendar grid and its keyboard behaviour. Option (c) shares the hard part and keeps the special cases out of the core (Chapter 05, tiers).

Case 2Marketing wants a "special" button for a campaign

The campaign team wants a large gradient button with an animated arrow for a two-week launch page.

Options

(a) Add a "campaign" variant to the core button. (b) Refuse. (c) Build it locally on the campaign page, using system primitives and tokens.

Recommendation

(c), with a quick review from the system designer for accessibility (contrast on the gradient, reduced-motion support).

Why

It's a one-off with a short life. Adding it to the core makes it everyone's maintenance forever; refusing makes the system look obstructive. A local build keeps both the campaign and the core healthy.

22.2Migrations: retiring the old and bringing in the new

Case 3Three dropdowns, one system: a migration plan

The three dropdowns from Chapter 15 are used about 450 times across four products. The system now has one good dropdown. How do you move everyone across without stopping product work?

Options

(a) A "migration sprint" where every team stops and switches. (b) Leave it and hope. (c) A phased plan with tooling and a clear end date.

Recommendation

(c). See the timeline below.

Why

Big-bang migrations compete with product deadlines and usually stall halfway. Phased migrations with automation and visible progress finish.

Case 4An acquired product built with a different technology

The company acquires a startup whose product uses a different front-end framework. Leadership wants it to "look like us" within six months.

Options

(a) Rewrite the acquired product in your framework. (b) Rebuild all system components in their framework. (c) Start with tokens, then the most visible components.

Recommendation

(c). Ship your tokens as CSS variables into their product first: colours, type and spacing change in weeks. Then restyle their existing buttons, inputs and navigation to use those tokens.

Why

Tokens work in any technology (Chapter 21). They deliver most of the visual consistency for a fraction of the cost, and buy time for a proper decision about the code later.

22.3Changes that could break things

Case 5Making every button 4 pixels taller

Research shows buttons are slightly too small on touch laptops. The fix is to increase the default height from 36 to 40 pixels.

Options

(a) Ship it as a patch; it's "just a visual tweak". (b) Add a new size and leave the default alone. (c) Treat it as a breaking change, with notice.

Recommendation

(c). Release it in the next major version, with before-and-after screenshots from the workshop, a test period in a pre-release, and a note telling teams which layouts to check (table rows, toolbars).

Why

Visual changes that affect layout can break products as surely as code changes.[[c:curtis-visual-breaking]] Option (b) avoids the problem today but leaves most of the product with the worse default.

Case 6Renaming a widely used token

The token color.text.muted is confusingly close to color.text.subtle. The team decides to merge them into color.text.secondary.

Options

(a) Rename and fix everything at once. (b) Keep both names forever. (c) Add the new name, point the old names at it, deprecate, then remove.

Recommendation

(c). The old names become aliases of the new one, so nothing changes visually. A lint rule suggests the new name; a codemod replaces it automatically.

Why

Aliases make renaming safe. The same deprecation path used for components (Chapter 16) works for tokens.

22.4When something goes wrong

Case 7An accessibility bug found in production

A customer reports that screen readers don't announce errors in the system's text field. It's used in every form, in every product.

What to do

Confirm it, fix it in the component, add a test that would have caught it, and ship a patch release the same week. Announce it in the system channel and release notes.

Then

Check which products are on old versions that won't get the fix, and help them update. If the issue affects a legal commitment, tell the relevant owners directly.

Why it's good news

This is the system working. One fix reaches every form. Without a system, it would be dozens of separate bugs in dozens of backlogs.

Case 8A component that ignores dark mode

After launching dark mode, three components stay bright. Investigation finds raw colours and primitive tokens in their styles.

Immediate fix

Map each part of the three components to the right semantic tokens (the anatomy map from Chapter 20) and release a patch.

Prevent it

Add lint rules that block raw colours and primitive tokens in component styles. Add dark mode to every visual test.

Why

The bug isn't the three components; it's the missing guardrail. Fix the class of problem, not just the instances.

22.5Pushing the limits

Case 9A table that must handle 10,000 rows

The analytics team needs a table with 10,000 rows, frozen columns and inline editing. The system's table handles a few hundred rows comfortably and has no editing.

Options

(a) Extend the core table to do everything. (b) A specialised data grid as a separate component. (c) A well-chosen third-party grid, styled with system tokens.

Recommendation

(b) or (c), depending on how many teams need it. Keep the core table simple. If only analytics needs this, (c) is usually the fastest, safest route.

Why

Spreadsheet-like grids are a level 4 problem of their own (Chapter 17). Stretching the simple table to do it would make it worse for the 90% of screens that just need a list.

Case 10Launching in Arabic and German

The company expands into the Middle East and Germany. Arabic reads right to left; German labels are often much longer than English ones.

What to check

Do components use logical properties so they mirror automatically?[[c:mdn-logical]] Do labels wrap or truncate gracefully? Do icons that imply direction (arrows) flip?

What to add

Right-to-left and long-text versions in every component's stress test (Chapter 14) and in visual tests.

Why now

Fixing direction and text length once in each component is far cheaper than fixing them in every screen of every product.

22.6Saying no, and listening

Case 11"Can we add a prop just for our screen?"

An engineer asks for a hideLabelOnMobile prop on the text field, for one compact filter bar.

Questions to ask

Do other teams need this? Is there an existing way (a visually hidden label that screen readers still read)? Would it cause accessibility problems?

Recommendation

Usually: show the existing accessible way to hide a label visually, and explain why the label must remain for screen readers. No new prop.

Why

One-team props are how components grow forty settings (Chapter 19). A helpful "here's how" beats both a new prop and a flat no.

Case 12A designer who detaches everything

Library data shows one designer detaching most components they use. It looks like they're ignoring the system.

The tempting reaction

Ask their manager to enforce the rules.

The better reaction

Sit with them for an hour. In our experience, it often turns out they work on the product area the system serves worst, and their detachments are a list of what's missing.

Why

Workarounds are feedback (Chapter 16). The heaviest "rule-breakers" are often your best source of requirements.

The pattern behind all twelve cases

Keep the core simple and strict. Give special needs a respectable home outside it. Change things with notice, tools and visible progress. And treat every workaround as information.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Shared core plus extensions handles most "different needs" without bloating components.
  2. Migrations finish when they're phased, automated and visible, not when they're big-bang.
  3. Treat layout-affecting visual changes and renames as breaking; use aliases and notice.
  4. Fix the guardrail, not just the bug, and listen to the people working around the system.
