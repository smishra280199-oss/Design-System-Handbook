Chapter 28

Team structure, roles and hierarchy

Who actually does the work of a design system, who do they report to, and who has the final say? This chapter builds a design system team from the ground up, covers how to operate with and without a dedicated team, and shows how the structure grows from a pair of people to a team of twenty.

Read this if

You're hiring for a design system, deciding where the team should report, or making a part-time model work without a dedicated team.

What you'll leave with
  1. Every design system role, what it does and when you need it.
  2. Team structures for three sizes, with organisation charts and reporting lines.
  3. How to run the system with no dedicated team, and how to transition when you outgrow that.

A mistake we see often

Hiring the designer, forgetting the engineer

We'll say it again because it's so common: a design system team without engineers produces a design tool library, not a system. Adoption happens in code. At every size, aim for at least as much engineering capacity as design capacity on the core team. [[prac]]

28.2Running the system without a dedicated team

Most companies start here, and many stay here for good reason. With no dedicated team, the system is looked after by people whose main job is product work. It can work well, but only if you're deliberate about it. Here's how to set it up so it doesn't quietly fade.

  1. Name two owners and protect their time

    One designer and one engineer, each with about 20% of their time (a day a week) explicitly reserved in their team's plan, and written into their goals. Unprotected time is no time.

  2. Form a small guild

    A group of interested designers and engineers from each team that meets every two weeks for 45 minutes: review new UI, decide what goes into the system, share problems.

  3. Keep a single backlog

    One visible list of system work, reviewed in the guild meeting. Items are picked up by whichever team needs them first, built properly, and contributed back.

  4. Rotate the support role

    A weekly rotation for answering questions in the system channel, so the two owners don't become the only point of contact.

  5. Build the system inside product work

    New components are built when a product feature needs them (the pilot idea from Chapter 05), with a little extra time to make them reusable.

No dedicated teamCheap; always grounded in real product needs; spreads knowledge widely; no "us and them"Product deadlines always win; slow on big work; nobody accountable for long-term quality; hard to do complex components or migrations

28.3Running the system with a dedicated team

A dedicated team changes the nature of the work. The system now has people whose success is measured by the system's success. That focus is powerful, but it also creates a risk: the team can drift away from the products it serves. The best dedicated teams operate like a small product company whose customers are internal.

What a dedicated team should do

  • Own a public roadmap, planned with product teams each quarter.
  • Ship on a regular rhythm, with release notes written for users.
  • Spend real time with product teams: pairing, pilots, office hours.
  • Run a support rota so questions get fast answers.
  • Review and merge contributions quickly.
  • Measure adoption, satisfaction and value, and report them.

What it should avoid

  • Building in isolation, then "launching" to teams who weren't involved.
  • Being the only people allowed to change anything.
  • Measuring success by components shipped rather than value delivered.
  • Saying yes to everything, or no to everything.
  • Spending more time on internal process than with product teams.

A useful rule of thumb from practice: a dedicated team should spend roughly a third of its time with product teams (pairing, supporting, researching), not only on its own backlog. [[prac]] If the team rarely sees product work, it will slowly build the wrong things.

28.5Where should the team report?

Reporting lines sound like an HR detail, but they shape what the system becomes. A team that reports only to design tends to drift towards Figma; one that reports only to engineering tends to drift towards code and away from product design quality.

Reports toTends to be good atWatch out for
Design leadershipVisual quality, foundations, design adoptionWeak engineering investment; code lags behind Figma
Engineering / platformCode quality, tooling, releases, technical adoptionDesign quality and design-tool library neglected
Product organisationPrioritising by business value; roadmap disciplineCan be treated as a feature team and reprioritised constantly
Joint (design and engineering)Balance; shared accountabilityNeeds two leaders who genuinely agree, or decisions stall

[[prac]] Our recommendation

Have one accountable leader for the system, and give designers and engineers on the team clear, equal standing, whichever organisation they formally sit in. A common working arrangement is a team that reports into design with engineers holding a dotted line to engineering leadership (or the reverse), plus a joint quarterly review with both design and engineering leaders.

28.6From part-time to dedicated: making the transition

The move from "looked after by product people" to "a dedicated team" is one of the riskiest moments in a system's life. Done badly, product teams feel the system has been taken away from them. Done well, it feels like the system finally got the support it needed.

  1. Make the case with evidence

    Use the signals from Chapter 27 and the numbers from Chapter 03: request wait times, local copies, time lost. Ask for the smallest team that solves the problem.

  2. Hire from inside where you can

    The part-time owners often make the best first dedicated members. They know the system, the history and the people.

  3. Keep the guild

    Turn the existing guild into the champions network. Contributors who built the system should keep a real voice in it.

  4. Announce what changes, and what doesn't

    New: faster answers, a roadmap, regular releases. Unchanged: anyone can still contribute and propose. Say both clearly.

  5. Show early wins

    In the first quarter, clear the oldest requests and fix the most annoying bugs. Trust is earned by response time.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Balance design and engineering on the team at every size, and add specialists as you grow.
  2. Without a dedicated team, protect owners' time, form a guild, keep one backlog and rotate support.
  3. A dedicated team should run like an internal product company and spend real time with product teams.
  4. Give the system one accountable leader, and plan the move from part-time to dedicated carefully.
