Chapter 23

Patterns: from components to solutions

Components are the words of your interface. Patterns are the sentences: proven ways of combining components to solve a problem people meet again and again, like filling in a form, finding something in a long list, or recovering from an error.

Read this if

Your product has consistent components but still feels inconsistent, or teams keep solving the same problems (forms, tables, empty screens) in different ways.

What you'll leave with
  1. What a pattern is, and how it differs from a component.
  2. Practical guidance for the most common patterns: forms, tables, navigation and feedback.
  3. How to document and look after patterns as part of the system.

23.1Words and sentences

You can have a perfect dictionary and still write confusing sentences. The same thing happens with design systems. Every team uses the system's button, text field and table, so the components are consistent, but each team combines them differently. One form shows errors at the top, another under each field, a third in a pop-up. One list paginates, another scrolls forever. The product still feels like several products.

New wordPatternA proven, documented way of combining components to solve a recurring user problem, such as "how we validate a form" or "how we show that there are no results".

That's what patterns fix. A pattern isn't a new component. It's guidance on how to use existing components together to solve a specific problem, with the reasoning and research behind it. The UK government's GOV.UK Design System is the best-known public example: alongside its components, it publishes patterns for things like asking for dates, addresses and names, each based on research with real users.[[c:govuk-patterns]] [[doc]]

ScenarioFour ways to say "nothing here"
The situation

A Ledgerly customer searches invoices, filters payments, opens an empty report and views a new project. Every component on those screens comes from the system.

What they see

"No data", a blank white area, a sad-face illustration with "Oops!", and "0 results found" in small grey text. Four teams, four different answers to the same question.

What a pattern changes

One documented empty-state pattern with three versions (first use, no results, cleared), each with a clear message and a next step. Same components, now saying the same thing.

23.2Forms

Forms are where products make money and where users get most frustrated, so they're usually the first pattern worth writing down. Here's the guidance we start from. [[prac]]

QuestionOur default answerWhy
LayoutOne column, labels above fieldsEasiest to scan; works on every screen size; labels stay close to their fields.
Required fieldsMark the optional ones ("optional") rather than starring the required ones, when most fields are requiredFewer marks, less noise; clear wording beats a symbol.
When to check for errorsWhen the person leaves a field (for format problems) and when they submit (for everything)Checking while someone is still typing shows errors before they've finished.
Where to show errorsA message next to each problem field, plus a summary at the top on submit that links to each errorPeople and screen readers can find every problem; the GOV.UK error summary is a well-tested model.[[c:govuk-errors]]
What errors sayWhat's wrong and how to fix it: "Enter a date after 1 January 2026"Errors must be identified and described in text.[[c:wcag-errors]] [[std]]
Long formsSplit into steps with a clear progress indicator, or into sections with headingsSmaller chunks feel achievable and reduce mistakes.
ButtonsOne primary action at the end, labelled with what it does ("Send invoice")The main action should be obvious and specific.

The point isn't that these answers are the only right ones. Some products have good reasons to differ. The point is that your product has one answer for each question, written down, so every form feels familiar and every team stops re-debating the same choices.

23.3Tables, lists and finding things

Most business software is, at heart, a collection of lists: invoices, customers, orders, users. How people find, sort and act on items in those lists decides how fast they can work.

Table, list or cards?

Table

When people compare

Many items with the same attributes, where people scan columns, sort and compare: invoices, transactions.

List

When people pick

Items with one or two key details, where people mostly find one and open it: messages, notifications.

Cards

When items are visual

Items where an image or a preview matters more than comparison: templates, projects, products.

Pagination, "load more" or endless scrolling?

ApproachBest forDownsides
PagesWork tools where people need to find their place again or reach the endExtra clicks; page size choice matters
"Load more" buttonFeeds and search results where people mostly look at the first fewLong pages; footer hard to reach after loading lots
Endless scrollingBrowsing content with no particular goalHard to return to a spot; footer unreachable; heavy for keyboard and screen-reader users

Search, filters and bulk actions

Write down how filters work across the product: where they sit (above the list, or in a side panel), whether results update instantly or after pressing "Apply", how active filters are shown and cleared, and what the empty result looks like. For bulk actions, decide once how people select many items (checkboxes, with a "select all on this page" option), where the actions appear (a bar that appears when items are selected), and how destructive bulk actions are confirmed. These small decisions, made consistently, are what make a large product feel learnable.

23.4Navigation

Navigation is the pattern people notice least when it works and most when it doesn't. The system should define the app frame (Chapter 10) and the rules for moving around inside it.

ComponentUse it forDon't use it for
Side menuThe main sections of an app with many areas (5+)Actions; settings for the current page
Top navigationA few main sections (up to about 5), often on websitesDeep hierarchies
TabsSwitching between views of the same thing (an invoice's details, payments, history)Moving between different areas of the app
BreadcrumbsShowing where you are in a deep hierarchy and stepping back upFlat products with one or two levels
Back linkReturning from a detail page or a step in a flowReplacing the browser's own back button behaviour

[[prac]] Three levels is usually enough

If people need to go more than three levels deep to reach something they use often (section, page, tab), the problem is usually the structure of the product, not the navigation component. That's a conversation for product leadership, and the design system is often the first to notice it.

23.5Feedback: loading, empty, error and success

Every screen has moments when things aren't normal: data is loading, there's nothing to show, something went wrong, or something just succeeded. These moments are where products most often feel inconsistent, because each team handles them as an afterthought.

Choosing how to tell people something

ComponentUse when the message is…Example
Inline messageAbout one specific field or section"Enter a full email address" under the field
ToastA short confirmation that needs no action, and that it's fine to miss"Invoice sent"
BannerAbout the whole page or account, and important enough to stay visible"Your trial ends in 3 days"
DialogSomething people must decide before they continue"Delete this customer and 12 invoices?"

Loading

For short waits, show a skeleton (grey shapes where the content will appear) so the page doesn't jump when it loads. For actions like saving, show progress on the button that was pressed. For long operations, show what's happening and, if possible, how long it will take. And always handle the case where loading fails, with a clear way to try again.

Empty states

First use

Nothing yet

Explain what will appear here and offer the first step: "No invoices yet. Create your first invoice to get paid faster."

No results

Search or filters found nothing

Say what was searched, and suggest changing or clearing filters.

All done

Everything is cleared

A small, positive confirmation: "All invoices are paid." No action needed.

23.6Documenting and looking after patterns

Patterns need documentation even more than components do, because there's no code to copy: the guidance is the pattern. A consistent template helps.

#SectionWhat it covers
1The problemWhat people are trying to do, in one or two sentences
2The solutionHow we solve it, which components are used, and in what arrangement
3ExamplesReal screens from your products, good ones and ones to improve
4VariationsApproved versions for different situations (for example, a short form vs a long one)
5When not to use itSituations where a different pattern fits better
6EvidenceResearch, test results or analytics that support the approach
7Accessibility and writingKeyboard flow, announcements, and the words to use

Patterns change more often than components, because they're closer to product decisions. Give each pattern an owner, often a senior product designer rather than the system team, and review patterns when research or analytics suggest something isn't working. When a pattern becomes stable and is built the same way everywhere, it may be worth turning into a "recipe" or composite component (Chapter 18), so teams can use it rather than rebuild it.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Patterns are documented ways of combining components to solve recurring problems.
  2. Start with forms, lists, navigation and feedback states; these cause the most inconsistency.
  3. Have one written answer to each recurring question, with reasons, and evidence where you have it.
  4. Document patterns with a consistent template, give each an owner, and promote stable ones into recipes.
