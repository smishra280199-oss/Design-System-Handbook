Chapter 29

Day-to-day operations

Org charts don't run design systems; routines do. This chapter is the practical how-to: the meetings, sessions and processes that keep a system moving every week, with how often each happens, who comes, who owns it and what it should produce.

Read this if

You run, or are about to run, a design system week to week and want a reliable set of routines.

What you'll leave with
  1. A complete operating calendar you can adapt.
  2. Formats for office hours, workshops, reviews and triage, with agendas.
  3. How to handle bugs, inconsistencies and debt, and how to plan across many product teams.

29.2Running office hours that people actually use

New wordOffice hoursA regular, open time slot where anyone can drop in with a question, a design or a piece of code and get help from the design system team.

Office hours are the single most effective routine a system team can run. They're cheap, they build relationships, and every session is free research: you hear exactly where people struggle. But many teams set them up, see three people turn up, and quietly stop. The difference between office hours that fade and office hours that thrive comes down to a few details.

The format

  • Weekly, same time, 60 minutes. Predictability matters more than length.
  • Two hosts: a system designer and a system engineer, so design and code questions can both be answered on the spot.
  • Optional sign-up with a one-line topic, so hosts can prepare, but drop-ins welcome.
  • Screens first: people bring the real file or the real code, not a description of it.
  • A second slot for distant time zones, or a recorded summary.

The outputs

  • Each question gets a resolution, or a follow-up owner and date.
  • Anything that reveals a gap becomes a logged request (Chapter 35).
  • Recurring questions become documentation or FAQ entries (Chapter 32).
  • A two-line weekly summary in the system channel: what came up, what changed.
ScenarioFrom three visitors to a waiting list
The situation

Ledgerly's office hours average three visitors. The team considers cancelling them.

What they change

They move the slot from Friday afternoon to Tuesday morning, add an engineer host, ask champions to bring one real problem each, and post a short summary after every session.

The result

Within two months, sessions are full and the summaries have become the channel's most-read posts. Half of the next quarter's roadmap items came from office hours.

By size: a startup doesn't need formal office hours; a standing "ask anything" thread works. A scale-up needs one weekly slot. Distributed and enterprise teams need several slots across time zones, sometimes specialised ones (for example "engineering office hours" and "accessibility clinic").

29.3Workshops and collaborative sessions

Office hours are reactive: people bring problems. Workshops are proactive: the system team brings people together to shape something before it's built. Three kinds are worth running regularly.

Component kick-off workshop

Run one at the start of every level 3 or level 4 component (Chapter 17). Invite the system designer and engineer, plus a designer and engineer from each team that will use it.

TimeActivityOutput
0–10 minThe problem: what are teams trying to do?Shared problem statement
10–30 minInventory walk-through: every existing version and its contextList of real use cases
30–55 minRequirements: must-haves, nice-to-haves, out of scopeDraft scope statement (Chapter 17)
55–75 minStates, edge cases and accessibility patternDraft checklist for design (Chapter 14)
75–90 minPilot team, timeline, ownersAgreed plan

Co-design and API review sessions

Once a first design exists, hold a 45-minute session where the system designer and engineer walk the requesting teams through the anatomy, props (Chapter 19) and states, and teams try to "break" it with their real cases. It's much cheaper to change a prop name in a review than after 400 screens use it.

Hands-on usage workshops

For new or complex components, a practical session where people build a real screen with the component, guided by the system team. These double as enablement (Chapter 30) and as usability testing of the component itself (Chapter 31).

29.4Reviews and feedback sessions

Reviews are where quality is protected. Too few and quality slips; too many and the system becomes a bottleneck. The trick is matching the review to the size of the change, the same principle as contribution levels in Chapter 05.

Contribution reviewCode and design contributions from product teamsOne reviewer for small; pair for largeAsynchronous, within 2–3 days

The product UI review deserves special mention. When a system designer regularly sits in on product teams' design critiques, they see how the system is really used, spot workarounds early, and build relationships. It's one of the cheapest forms of research and enablement at the same time.

29.5Weekly triage and prioritising across teams

Chapter 35 covers the full request framework. Day to day, the key routine is a weekly triage: 45 minutes where the lead, a system designer and a system engineer go through every new request and give each one an answer. The rule is simple: no request waits more than a week for a first response, even if the response is "not this quarter, and here's why".

  1. Read and clarify

    What's the actual need? Is there enough information? If not, ask the requester a specific question.

  2. Categorise

    Bug, enhancement, new component, new pattern, documentation, question. Then: shared need, or product-specific?

  3. Decide the route

    Fix now; add to backlog with a priority; invite a contribution; suggest an existing solution; build locally.

  4. Reply and record

    Post the decision on the request with a reason and, if accepted, a rough timeframe. Update the public roadmap if needed.

When several product teams compete for the same small team's time, prioritise in the open. Publish the scoring (Chapter 35), show the queue, and explain trade-offs. Teams accept "not yet" far more easily when they can see why, and what's ahead of them.

29.6Bugs, inconsistencies, dependencies and debt

Things will go wrong, and a system that handles problems quickly earns more trust than one that seems never to have any. Define response times by severity, publish them, and stick to them.

SeverityExampleFirst responseFix target
CriticalA component blocks a key task, or a serious accessibility failure in productionSame dayPatch release within days
HighVisible bug in a common component; broken in one browser1 working dayNext release
MediumEdge case broken; minor visual inconsistencyWithin triage weekWithin the quarter
LowCosmetic issue; documentation typoWithin triage weekWhen convenient, or invite a contribution

Response and fix targets are [[prac]] starting points; adjust them to your team's size.

Inconsistencies (places where the product doesn't match the system) are different from bugs. Log them separately, because many are product teams' to fix, and fixing them is part of adoption. A shared "inconsistency list" that product teams pick from during quieter weeks works well.

Dependencies (frameworks, libraries and tools the system relies on) need routine upkeep. Schedule upgrades quarterly rather than waiting until something breaks, and check what depends on what before upgrading (Chapter 18).

Debt is managed with the visible debt list and a fixed slice of capacity described in Chapter 05.

29.7Planning cycles

System teams serve many masters, so planning needs to be visible and shared. Most teams combine a quarterly roadmap with short delivery cycles (two-week sprints or a continuous flow).

Quarterly planning, step by step

  1. Two weeks before: collect needs from product teams' roadmaps and from research (Chapter 31).
  2. Score candidate work with the request framework (Chapter 35).
  3. Draft a roadmap with capacity split across the buckets on the right.
  4. Review with product and design leads; adjust.
  5. Publish on the documentation portal and announce it (Chapter 33).

A healthy capacity split [[prac]]

Roadmap work
50%
Support & enablement

20%
Improvement & research

Teams that plan 100% roadmap work always miss their roadmap, because support and bugs arrive anyway.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Give the system a predictable heartbeat, and make every recurring meeting produce a clear output.
  2. Run office hours weekly with a designer and an engineer, and turn what you hear into requests and docs.
  3. Use workshops before complex components, match reviews to the size of the change, and triage every request weekly.
  4. Publish severity targets for bugs, and plan quarterly with a capacity split that leaves room for support and debt.
