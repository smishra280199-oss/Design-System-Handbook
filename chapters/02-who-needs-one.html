Chapter 02

Who needs a design system, and when

Every product already has a design system. It just might be implicit, inconsistent and stored in people's heads. The real question is how much of it to make explicit, and when.

Read this if

You are deciding whether to invest now, later or not at all, or you are being asked to justify a system to someone who thinks it is premature.

What you'll leave with
  1. Direct answers to the seven questions founders and leaders ask most.
  2. A five-stage maturity model to place your organisation, and to see the next stage.
  3. A decision tree and readiness checklist you can use in a planning meeting.

02.1The seven questions everyone asks

These answers are direct by design. Each one names the condition under which it would change.

Does every company need a design system?

Every company needs consistent design decisions. Not every company needs a dedicated, formal system.

Any product with more than one screen has made decisions about colour, type, spacing and behaviour. The question is whether those decisions are written down, shared and reusable, or re-made by whoever builds the next screen. A two-person team with one product can stay consistent through conversation. Once decisions are made by people who don't talk daily, implicit systems start to fork. [[prac]]

Is a design system only for enterprises?

No. Small companies run them too, at a smaller scale.

zeroheight's 2025 survey of design system practitioners found dedicated teams averaging about three people at companies with fewer than 100 employees, rising to about nine at companies with 500+.[[c:zeroheight-2025]] [[doc]] (The sample is self-selected: people who answer a design systems survey tend to have one.) Enterprises need more governance, not more system. The core of a small system and a large one is the same: foundations, tokens and the components you use most.

Does a startup need product–market fit first?

Before product–market fit, build foundations, not a system.

Pre-PMF, the product will change shape, so investing in a broad component library and governance is waste. But a token layer (named colours, a type scale, a spacing scale) costs days, not months, and it makes pivots cheaper: restyling a product built on tokens is a configuration change. Use an off-the-shelf component library (Radix, MUI and similar) themed with your own tokens, and defer custom components until the product stabilises. [[prac]]

Is a team of 5–6 designers big enough?

Usually yes. But count engineers and surfaces, not designers.

Five designers on one product with eight engineers can stay coherent with a well-maintained UI kit and a shared token file. Five designers serving 40 engineers across a web app, a mobile app and an admin tool cannot. They will spend their week answering "which grey is it?" in Slack. The trigger for a real system is the number of places UI gets built, and how far apart they are. [[prac]]

When is a UI kit enough instead of a full system?

When the problem is design consistency, not delivery consistency.

  • One product, one platform, one codebase, and fewer than about three squads.
  • Engineering already uses a themed third-party component library.
  • Designers disagree with each other more than design disagrees with code.

A UI kit fixes design-to-design drift. It does nothing for design-to-code drift. When the second problem appears, the kit has reached its ceiling.

How do we know we've outgrown what we have?

When the cost of inconsistency shows up in delivery, not just in design reviews.

The signals checklist in 02.4 is the practical test. If you tick five or more, the case for a system is already being paid for, just in rework instead of in investment.

When is a design system unnecessary or premature?

When nobody will own it, nobody will adopt it, or the product won't live long enough to benefit.

  • You are still searching for the product and expect to rebuild the UI within six months.
  • One designer and one or two engineers build everything, and they sit together.
  • The product is a marketing site or a campaign with a short life.
  • Engineering leadership has not agreed to adopt it. A design-only system is a UI kit with a bigger budget.
  • No one can be given protected time to own it. An unowned system decays faster than no system, because people stop trusting it.

02.2A maturity model: where you are, and what's next

Design system maturity is a staircase, not a switch. Most organisations should aim for the next stage, not the last one. Skipping stages usually means building governance for components nobody uses yet.

02.3The decision tree

If you only have ten minutes in a leadership meeting, use this. It intentionally asks about engineering and surfaces before it asks about design.

02.4Signs you have outgrown your current UI infrastructure

Each of these is observable, and most can be counted. Count them before you build the business case; they will become your baseline metrics in Chapter 03.

In the code

  • A search for hard-coded hex values returns hundreds of unique colours.
  • There is more than one Button (or Modal, or Table) component in the codebase.
  • The same accessibility bug (focus, contrast, labels) has been fixed in several places.
  • Engineers estimate a brand colour change in weeks, not hours.
  • Front-end PR reviews regularly argue about spacing and styling.

In the organisation

  • Design QA has become a release gate that blocks shipping.
  • New designers or engineers take weeks to learn "how we do UI here".
  • Customers or sales mention that products "feel like different companies".
  • A second product, platform, brand or acquisition is on the roadmap.
  • Enterprise deals now include accessibility conformance requirements.

[[prac]] How to read your score

0–2: keep doing what you're doing; write down your foundations. 3–5: you are paying for inconsistency in rework; plan Stage 2. 6+: the system is already costing you more by its absence than it would by its presence. Start with an audit to size it.

02.5Four scenarios

These are composite, fictional scenarios. They are not clients; they illustrate how the decision tree resolves in different contexts. [[ill]]

Scenario A — Seed fintech

2 designers, 6 engineers, pre-PMF

Situation: one web app, pivoting monthly. The founder wants a "design system like Stripe's".

Recommendation: no system yet. Spend a week defining colour, type and spacing tokens; theme an open-source component library. Why: the product will change, and the tokens will survive the change while bespoke components won't.

Scenario B — Series B SaaS

7 designers, 45 engineers, 2 products

Situation: a web app plus a newly acquired analytics product with its own UI stack. Three button styles; dark mode requested by customers.

Recommendation: Stage 2→3. A two-week audit, then a semantic token layer that themes both products, and 15–20 shared components. Core team of ~2 FTE plus named contributors.

Scenario C — Enterprise internal tools

30+ tools built by different vendors

Situation: every tool looks different; accessibility is a compliance requirement; nobody owns UI centrally.

Recommendation: start with tokens and an accessibility baseline, not components. Tokens can be retro-fitted into legacy stacks; components can't. Establish a hybrid team model and a contribution path vendors must follow.

Scenario D — Brand and marketing site

Agency-built, redesigned every 2–3 years

Situation: the marketing team asks for a design system for the website.

Recommendation: a style guide plus a small set of CMS blocks is the right tool. Share colour and type tokens with the product so the brand stays aligned, but don't fund a product-grade system for a site with a short life.

02.6Readiness checklist

Use this before committing budget. A "no" doesn't mean don't proceed. It means that item becomes the first workstream.

  • Sponsor. A leader with budget authority who will defend the system in planning.
  • Engineering commitment. Engineering leadership agrees teams will adopt the package.
  • Owner. A named person with protected time; ownership is in the job description.
  • Audit. You know what exists today: styles, components, tokens, a11y gaps.
  • Pilot team. One product team volunteers to build with the system first.
  • Baseline metrics. You've counted the signals in 02.4, so you can show change.
  • Scope. You've chosen the next stage, not the final one.
  • Tech stack. You know which frameworks and platforms the system must output to.
