Chapter 02

Who needs a design system, and when

Here's a secret: every product already has a design system. It's just usually unwritten, inconsistent and living in a few people's heads. The real question isn't "do we need one?" It's "how much of it should we write down, and when?"

Read this if

You're trying to decide whether to invest now, later or not at all, or someone is telling you it's too early.

What you'll leave with
  1. Straight answers to the seven questions founders and leaders ask most.
  2. A simple way to tell where your company is today and what the next step looks like.
  3. A decision tree and a checklist you can bring to a planning meeting.

02.1The seven questions everyone asks

When we sit down with founders and product leaders, the same seven questions come up almost every time. Here are our honest answers, including when the answer would change.

Does every company need a design system?

Every company needs consistent design decisions. Not every company needs a formal system with its own team.

If your product has more than one screen, you've already made decisions about colours, text and spacing. The question is whether those decisions are written down and shared, or re-made by whoever builds the next screen. Two people sitting side by side can stay consistent just by talking. Once decisions are being made by people who don't talk every day, the unwritten system starts to split. [[prac]]

Isn't this only for big enterprises?

No. Small companies run them too, just smaller.

zeroheight's 2025 survey of people who work on design systems found teams averaging about three people at companies with fewer than 100 employees, and about nine at companies with 500 or more.[[c:zeroheight-2025]] [[doc]] (Keep in mind that people who answer a design systems survey usually have one.) What big companies need more of isn't system, it's coordination: more teams, more rules about who decides what. The core of a small system and a large one looks surprisingly similar.

Should a startup wait until it finds product–market fit?

Wait on the big system. Don't wait on the basics.

Before product–market fit, your product will change shape, so building a large custom component library is likely wasted effort. But naming your colours, text sizes and spacing takes days, not months, and it actually makes pivots cheaper: restyling a product built on named values is a quick change instead of a rebuild. Pair that with a good open-source component library (Radix, MUI and others) styled with your own values, and save custom components for when the product settles down. [[prac]]

We have five or six designers. Is that enough to justify one?

Usually yes, but count engineers and products, not just designers.

Five designers on one product with eight engineers can stay consistent with a good UI kit and a shared list of named values. Five designers supporting forty engineers across a web app, a mobile app and an admin panel cannot. They'll spend half their week answering "which grey is it?" in Slack. What really drives the need is how many places UI is being built, and how far apart those places are. [[prac]]

When is a UI kit enough?

When designers disagreeing with each other is the main problem, rather than design and code disagreeing.

A UI kit is often enough if you have one product, on one platform, built in one codebase, by fewer than about three squads, and engineering already uses a well-styled off-the-shelf component library. It stops designs drifting apart. It does nothing to stop code drifting away from design. When that second problem shows up, you've hit the ceiling.

How do we know we've outgrown what we have?

When inconsistency starts costing you time in delivery, not just debates in design reviews.

The checklist in 02.4 is a practical test. If you tick five or more items, you're already paying for a design system. You're just paying for it in rework instead of investment.

When is it too early, or unnecessary?

When nobody will own it, nobody will use it, or the product won't live long enough to benefit.

Hold off if you expect to rebuild your UI from scratch within six months; if one designer and one or two engineers build everything together; if it's a short-lived marketing site; or if engineering leadership hasn't agreed to use it. A system that only designers use is just an expensive UI kit. And be honest about ownership. A system nobody looks after decays faster than having no system at all, because people stop trusting it and start working around it.

02.2Where are you today? A five-stage map

Design systems grow in stages, like a staircase. The most useful thing you can do is figure out which step you're on and aim for the next one, not the top. Jumping straight to the top usually means building approval processes for components nobody uses yet.

StageYou'll recognise it when…The next stepWhat it takes
0 UnwrittenDesigners ask each other for hex codes; engineers measure screenshots.Write down the basics; publish a UI kit.1–3 weeks of one designer
1 Shared stylesThe designs look consistent, but the code is full of colours that "look about right".Name the values once and share them with code; build the most-used components.1–3 months, a designer and an engineer, part-time
2 Basics in codeNew screens use the package, older ones don't, and nobody owns incoming requests.Give it owners, documentation, releases and a way to contribute.A small dedicated team (1.5–3 people)

4 PlatformThe system is treated like infrastructure, with its own roadmap and targets.Keep it healthy; make change cheaper; automate checks.An ongoing product budget

These stages describe your company's relationship with its design system. Ben Callahan at Sparkbox describes a complementary maturity model for the system itself, which starts with "building version one" and then "growing adoption" before the system settles into a long-lived product.[[c:callahan-maturity]] The two views fit together well. Whatever stage your company is at, the system goes through a hard early phase where it has to prove it's worth using. Chapter 05 covers that phase in detail.

02.4Signs you've outgrown what you have

You don't need a consultant to tell you whether you have a consistency problem. Most of the evidence is sitting in your code and your calendar. Here's what to look for, and every item can be counted or observed, which is useful because these become your "before" numbers later.

In the code

  • A search for colour codes finds hundreds of different colours.
  • There's more than one button component (or dialog, or table) in the codebase.
  • The same accessibility bug has been fixed in several places.
  • Engineers think a brand colour change would take weeks, not hours.
  • Code reviews regularly turn into debates about spacing and styling.

In the organisation

  • Design review has become a bottleneck before every release.
  • New designers or engineers take weeks to learn "how we do UI here".
  • Customers or sales say your products feel like different companies.
  • A second product, platform, brand or acquisition is on the roadmap.
  • Enterprise customers now ask for accessibility compliance.
ScenarioThe dark mode request that exposed everything
The situation

A B2B product gets its most-requested feature for the third quarter running: dark mode. The PM estimates two sprints.

What goes wrong

Engineering finds 340 different colour values typed directly into the code, many of them nearly identical greys. There's no way to "switch" them, because nothing says which grey is for text and which is for borders. The estimate becomes two quarters.

What a design system changes

With colours named by their job ("text", "border", "background"), dark mode is a second set of values for the same names. It becomes a design task and a testing task, not a rewrite. Chapter 07 shows exactly how.

[[prac]] How to read your score

0–2 ticks: you're fine for now; write down your basics. 3–5: you're paying for inconsistency in rework, so plan the next stage. 6 or more: not having a system is already costing you more than having one would. Start with an audit to see the size of the problem.

02.5Four companies, four different answers

To make this concrete, here are four situations we see again and again. They're composites, not real clients, but you'll probably recognise one of them. [[ill]]

Company AA fintech startup: 3 designers, 4 engineers, still finding its feet

The founder has seen Stripe's design and wants "a proper design system". The product changes direction every month or two. Everyone sits in one room.

What they need

The basics only: about a week to name colours, text sizes and spacing, plus a good open-source component library styled to match.

Why not more?

Custom components would be redesigned with each pivot. The named values survive pivots; the components wouldn't.

When to revisit

When a second squad forms, or when they start building a mobile app.

Company BA Series B SaaS: 7 designers, 45 engineers, 2 products

They have their main web app and a recently acquired analytics product built with completely different code. There are three button styles in production, and customers keep asking for dark mode.

What they need

A two-week audit, then a shared set of named values that can style both products, and 15–20 shared components.

Who does it

A small core team of about two people, plus a named designer and engineer in each squad who contribute.

The payoff

Dark mode and visual consistency across both products, and a path to merge the acquired product's UI over time.

Company CAn enterprise with 30+ internal tools built by different vendors

Every tool looks different. Accessibility is now a legal requirement. Nobody owns the interface centrally, and each vendor uses its own technology.

What they need

Start with named values and accessibility rules, not components. Those can be added to old tools; new components can't be dropped into every old technology.

Who does it

A central team that sets the rules, with a clear process vendors must follow to contribute and get approval.

The payoff

A consistent, accessible baseline across tools, without rewriting them all at once.

Company DA marketing website, rebuilt every two or three years

The marketing team asks for "a design system for the website". An agency builds and maintains it.

What they need

A style guide and a set of reusable page sections in the CMS.

The smart move

Share the same named colours and fonts with the product, so the brand stays aligned between website and app.

What to avoid

Funding a product-grade system for a site that will be thrown away in two years.

02.6Are you ready? A checklist

Run through this before you commit budget. A "no" doesn't mean stop. It means that item becomes the first thing to work on.

  • A sponsor. A leader with budget who will defend the system in planning meetings.
  • Engineering on board. Engineering leadership agrees teams will use the shared code.
  • An owner. A named person with protected time, written into their role.
  • An audit. You know what exists today: styles, components, values, accessibility gaps.
  • A pilot team. One product team volunteers to build with it first.
  • "Before" numbers. You've counted the signs in 02.4, so you can show progress.
  • The right scope. You've picked the next stage, not the last one.
  • Known technology. You know which platforms and frameworks it has to support.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Every product has a design system; the choice is how much of it to write down and share.
  2. What drives the need is how many places UI gets built, more than how many designers you have.
  3. Before product–market fit, name your basic values and use an off-the-shelf library. Hold off on the big custom system.
  4. Aim for the next stage, not the final one, and don't start without engineering leadership on board.
