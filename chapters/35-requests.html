Chapter 35

Request management and contribution

Ten teams, forty requests, one small system team. Handling many requests at once is one of the hardest parts of running a design system. This chapter gives you a complete framework: how requests come in, how they're judged and prioritised, how urgent cases and exceptions work, and how each request is tracked to the end.

Read this if

Requests are piling up, teams complain that the system is slow or unfair, or you need a clear, defensible way to decide what gets built.

What you'll leave with
  1. An intake process and request form that captures what you need.
  2. A test for shared versus product-specific needs, and a scoring model for priorities.
  3. A full request lifecycle, plus rules for urgent requests, exceptions and contributions.

35.1The problem with "just ask us"

In the early days, requests arrive informally: a message here, a hallway chat there. That works until about the third product team. Then requests get lost, the loudest team gets served first, and quiet teams decide the system isn't for them.

ScenarioWhoever shouts loudest
The situation

Requests reach Ledgerly's system team through Slack, direct messages, meetings and a spreadsheet. A senior product manager escalates their team's request to a director.

What goes wrong

The escalated request jumps the queue. Two quieter teams who asked months earlier hear nothing. One gives up and builds its own components; the other complains that the system is "political".

What a framework changes

One front door, a public queue, a published scoring method and a weekly triage. Priorities can still change, but openly, with reasons everyone can see.

A request framework isn't bureaucracy. It's fairness made visible. It protects the system team from being pulled in ten directions, and it protects product teams from being forgotten.

35.2One front door: submitting a request

All requests go through one place: a form on the portal that creates an item in a public tracker (GitHub issues, Jira or similar). Questions can still be asked in chat, but anything that needs work becomes a request. A good form takes two minutes to fill in and gives the system team everything it needs.

REQUEST FORM portal → tracker
1. What kind of request?

Bug · Enhancement to an existing component · New component · New pattern · Token change · Documentation · Other

2. What problem are you trying to solve?

Describe the user need, not the solution. "Customers can't tell which invoices are overdue" rather than "add a red badge".

3. Where does it appear?

Product, screen, and a screenshot or Figma link.

4. What have you tried?

Existing components you considered and why they didn't fit.

5. Who else might need this?

Other teams or products you know of.

6. When do you need it, and why?

The deadline and what depends on it. "Launch on 12 March" helps; "ASAP" doesn't.

7. Could your team help build it?

Yes / Maybe / No. Contributions get faster turnaround.

Question 2 is the most important. Teams usually arrive with a solution ("add a red badge"), but the system team needs the problem, because the best answer might be an existing component, a pattern, or something more general that helps other teams too.

35.3Shared need or product-specific?

The single most important judgement in request management: does this belong in the system, or in the product? Get it wrong one way and the system bloats with one-offs; get it wrong the other way and teams duplicate work. Use these five questions in triage.

#QuestionPoints towards the system if…Points towards the product if…
1How many teams need it?Two or more now, or clearly soon (the rule of two)Only one team
2Is the need general?Describes a common problem (show status, pick a date range)Tied to one product's domain (a loan amortisation chart)
3Does it duplicate something?No existing component or pattern solves itAn existing one does, with small changes or better guidance
4Will it last?Long-lived needA campaign, experiment or short-lived feature
5Is it consistent with the system?Fits the principles and foundationsRequires breaking them

These questions echo the GOV.UK Design System's public criteria, where proposals must show they're useful and unique before they're accepted.[[c:govuk-criteria]] Nathan Curtis has written a whole article on contribution criteria for the same reason.[[c:curtis-criteria]] When the answer is "product-specific", the team builds it locally from system parts (Chapter 13), and the request stays open as a "watch" item. If a second team asks, it becomes a candidate for the system.

35.4Categorising and prioritising

Every request accepted for the system gets a category and a score. The score doesn't decide automatically, but it makes trade-offs visible and discussions much calmer. Here's the scoring model we use. [[prac]]

FactorQuestionScore 1 · 2 · 3
ReachHow many teams or screens will benefit?1 team · 2–4 teams · 5+ teams
ImpactHow much does it help each team or user?Nice to have · saves real time · unblocks or fixes a serious problem
ReuseWill it be reused in future work?Rarely · sometimes · constantly
Business valueDoes it support a company priority, revenue, compliance or accessibility?Indirect · supports a priority · required for one
Effort (divides)How complex is it? (Chapter 17)Level 1–2 · level 3 · level 4

Priority score = (Reach + Impact + Reuse + Business value) ÷ Effort. A bug fix used everywhere scores high; a complex component for one team scores low. Publish the scores in the public queue, next to each request.

35.5Competing priorities, urgent requests and exceptions

When teams compete

When two teams both want their request done first, the scores start the conversation, but people finish it. Three habits help. Make the queue public, so everyone sees what's ahead. Agree a quarterly roadmap with product leads (Chapter 29), so most priorities are settled once, together. And when a priority genuinely changes mid-quarter, say what's moving down to make room, and tell the affected team directly.

Urgent requests

Some requests really are urgent: a launch next week, a legal deadline, a critical accessibility failure. Define "urgent" clearly and give it a fast lane, or everything will be called urgent.

Qualifies as urgentFast-lane response
A system bug blocking a launch or a key user taskFix within days as a patch (Chapter 29 severity)
A legal, regulatory or accessibility deadlinePrioritised above roadmap work, with leadership informed
A launch date with no time for the normal processBuild locally now, from system parts; system version follows; local version replaced later

Exceptions

Sometimes a team needs to break a system rule: a different colour for a partner integration, a component that can't meet a guideline yet. Allow exceptions, but make them explicit: a short written request, a named approver (Chapter 36), an expiry date and an entry in the exception log. Review the log quarterly. Recurring exceptions are a sign the system needs to change.

[[prac]] The system should never be the reason a team misses a deadline

If the system can't deliver in time, the answer is a sanctioned local build, not a delay. That one promise does more for trust than any process.

35.6Contribution guidelines and review

When a team offers to build something themselves, the request becomes a contribution. Chapter 05 introduced four levels of contribution. Here's what the guidelines should say for each, so contributors know exactly what's expected.

LevelContributor providesSystem team providesReview
1 · ReportA clear description with a screenshotTriage within a week—
2 · FixThe fix, a test, a changelog lineReview within 2–3 daysOne reviewer
3 · ExtendA short proposal; then design, code, tests and docsProposal feedback; pairing if wantedDesigner + engineer
4 · NewA written proposal with evidence of shared need; co-buildingWorkshop (Chapter 29), co-design, final reviewDesign review + code review + accessibility

All contributions meet the same definition of done as system-built work (Chapter 21). The difference is in support, not standards. Contributors shouldn't get a lower bar; they should get more help reaching it.

35.7The request lifecycle

Every request moves through the same stages, visible to everyone in the tracker. The requester always knows where their request is and what happens next.

Notice that a request isn't finished when it's released; it's finished when the requesting team is using it. Tracking requests to adoption closes a gap many teams miss, where something ships and the team who asked for it never switches over.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Use one front door and a public queue; ask for the problem, not the solution.
  2. Test every request for shared versus product-specific need; send one-offs to local builds and watch them.
  3. Score requests openly by reach, impact, reuse and value against effort; define urgent and handle exceptions explicitly.
  4. Track every request from submission to adoption, with a named owner and a service target at each stage.
