Chapter 33

Communication and change management

Every change to a design system lands in someone else's product. How you announce it decides whether teams feel supported or ambushed. This chapter covers the channels, the audiences, the timing and the exact words, with templates you can copy.

Read this if

You announce releases, plan breaking changes or deprecations, or teams keep saying "nobody told us".

What you'll leave with
  1. A channel setup for Slack or Microsoft Teams, email and the portal.
  2. Who communicates what, to whom, and when.
  3. Ready-to-use templates for releases, breaking changes, deprecations, new components, token updates and fixes.

33.1Communication is part of the product

Scenario"We found out from a broken build"
The situation

The system team renames a prop in a major release. It's mentioned in the release notes, posted once in a busy channel on a Friday evening.

What goes wrong

Three teams update on Monday as part of routine maintenance. Their builds break. One is mid-launch. The incident review concludes: "the design system broke production".

What should have happened

Notice weeks ahead, a direct message to affected teams' champions, a migration guide and codemod, a clear "breaking" label, and a release on a Tuesday morning with the team on hand.

In that story, the code change was fine. The communication failed. That's typical: most of the anger product teams feel towards design systems comes from surprises, not from the changes themselves. Communication is part of what the system team delivers, just like components.

33.2Channels, and what each one is for

The most common mistake is one channel for everything: announcements, questions, debates and jokes all mixed together, so important news scrolls away in minutes. Separate channels by purpose. The names below use Slack conventions; the same setup works in Microsoft Teams with channels in one team.

ChannelPurposeRules
#ds-announcementsReleases, breaking changes, deprecations, roadmap updatesOnly the system team posts; reactions allowed; questions go to threads or #ds-help
#ds-helpQuestions and supportAnyone posts; answered within a working day by the support rota
#ds-communityDiscussion, ideas, show-and-tell, patternsAnyone posts; light moderation (Chapter 34)
#ds-championsEarly previews and coordination with championsChampions + system team
Email digestMonthly summary for people who don't live in chat, especially leadersOne email a month, short
Portal: UpdatesThe permanent record of every changeEvery announcement links here (Chapter 32)
Leadership reportQuarterly outcomes, metrics and asksOne page (Chapter 37)

One rule ties these together: chat is for attention, the portal is for record. Every announcement in chat links to the full details on the portal, so nothing important lives only in a message that will scroll away.

33.3Who communicates, and who needs to hear

Different people need different messages. A designer needs to know about a new Figma component; an engineering manager needs to know about a breaking change that affects their sprint; a VP needs to know whether the system is delivering value.

Message typeSent byMust reachThrough
Release (minor, patch)Engineer on release rotaDesigners and engineers using the system#ds-announcements, portal
Breaking changeSystem leadEngineers and engineering managers of affected teams; championsAnnouncements + direct message to affected teams + portal + digest
DeprecationComponent ownerTeams using the component (from usage data)Announcements + direct to affected teams + warnings in code and Figma
New component or patternSystem designerAll designers and engineersAnnouncements + demo + digest
Token updateSystem designer + engineerEveryone, if visible; engineers, if names changeAnnouncements, with before/after
Critical bug fixEngineer on rotaTeams affectedAnnouncements + direct message
RoadmapSystem leadProduct, design and engineering leads; everyonePortal + quarterly announcement + digest
Outcomes and asksSystem leadLeadership and sponsorsQuarterly report

The champions network (Chapter 34) is the secret weapon here. For anything important, brief the champions a day early in their own channel. They'll make sure their teams hear it, in their own words, at the right moment.

33.4When, and how often

Predictable timing is itself a form of communication. When teams know releases come every other Tuesday and breaking changes are announced at least one release cycle ahead, they can plan, and they stop being surprised.

WhatWhen
Minor and patch releasesOn the regular release day; early in the working week, never late on a Friday
Breaking change: first noticeAt least one full release cycle before, and ideally 4–8 weeks, with the migration guide ready [[prac]]
Breaking change: remindersOne week before, and on release day
DeprecationWhen marked deprecated; again at the halfway point; one week before removal
Monthly digestFirst week of each month
RoadmapStart of each quarter, plus whenever it changes significantly
Critical fixesImmediately

A mistake we see often

Announcing too much

Posting every small change creates noise, and people mute the channel, then miss the one breaking change that matters. Bundle small changes into the regular release post, keep the announcements channel for things people need to know, and use clear labels (BREAKING, NEW, FIX) so readers can skim.

33.6Handling feedback on changes

Announcements invite reactions: questions, concerns and sometimes strong objections. Handle them well and people feel heard, even when the change goes ahead. Handle them badly and every future change becomes a fight.

  1. Discuss before deciding, not after

    For significant changes, publish a short proposal (Chapter 05's RFC) for comments before the decision. Announcements should rarely be the first time people hear about something big.

  2. Keep discussion in threads

    Questions about an announcement go in its thread, so the context stays together and the announcements channel stays readable.

  3. Answer within a day, and collect the answers

    Recurring questions go into the change's FAQ on the portal.

  4. Take objections seriously

    If a team has a real blocker (a launch, a technical constraint), offer options: more time, help with migration, or a temporary exception (Chapter 35). Record what was agreed.

  5. Report back

    After a big change, share how it went: how many teams migrated, what went wrong, what you'll do differently.

33.7Keeping distributed teams informed

When teams span time zones, every live-only moment excludes someone. A few habits keep everyone equally informed.

  • Written first. Every decision, announcement and change exists in writing on the portal before it's discussed live.
  • Record everything. Demos, community sessions and walkthroughs are recorded and posted with a short summary.
  • Rotate times. Live sessions alternate between time zones, or run twice.
  • Release when people are awake. Ship at a time that falls within working hours for most regions, with someone from the system team available.
  • Regional champions. A champion in each region relays important news locally and in local context.
  • Give longer notice. Breaking changes need more lead time across regions, because coordination is slower.

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Most frustration with design systems comes from surprises, so communication is part of the product.
  2. Separate channels by purpose; chat is for attention, the portal is for record.
  3. Match messages to audiences, brief champions first, and give breaking changes weeks of notice.
  4. Use consistent templates, handle feedback in threads, and work written-first across time zones.
