Chapter 05

Running a design system day to day

Building a design system is the easy part. Running it is where most of them succeed or fail. Who owns it? Who decides what goes in? What happens when two teams want different things, or an update breaks someone's screen? This chapter is about the everyday operation.

Read this if

You're setting up a system team, your system isn't being used as much as you hoped, or product teams have started calling the system team "the bottleneck".

What you'll leave with
  1. A clear picture of the ways companies organise design system ownership, and which fits you.
  2. A practical process for requests, contributions, updates and breaking changes.
  3. Ways to get people using the system, and to keep it healthy for years.

05.1It's a product, not a project

A project has an end date. You build it, you launch it, you move on. A lot of design systems are treated this way, and that's exactly why they fail. Nathan Curtis, one of the most influential voices in the field, put it in a phrase that has stuck for a decade: a design system isn't a project, it's a product, serving products.[[c:curtis-product]]

What does "product" mean in practice? It means the system has users, who are the designers and engineers in your product teams. It has a roadmap of what's coming next. It ships updates on a regular rhythm. It offers support when people get stuck. And somebody checks whether it's actually working for the people who use it. Ben Callahan of Sparkbox describes the people who build with a system as its "subscribers", and that's a useful way to think about them. They chose to rely on you. Keep earning it.[[c:callahan-maturity]]

Product habitWhat it looks like for a design system
Know your usersRegularly sit with product teams. Look at what they build outside the system, and ask why.
Keep a roadmapA public list of what's coming, ordered by how many teams need it and how painful the gap is.
Ship on a rhythmRegular updates with clear notes on what changed, so teams can plan.
Offer supportA Slack channel with someone answering, weekly office hours, and a short onboarding session for new joiners.
Measure what mattersIs it being used? Is it saving time? Do people like working with it? (See 05.8.)

[[prac]] A simple test

Ask a designer in a product team: "If the system doesn't have what you need, what do you do?" If they can answer confidently, you have an operating model. If they shrug and say "I just make my own", you have a library.

05.2Who owns it? Four ways to organise

Every company that runs a design system has to answer one question early: whose job is it? In his 2015 essay on team models, Nathan Curtis described the options that most of the industry still uses today.[[c:curtis-team-models]] Here they are, in plain terms.

One team's system (Curtis calls it "solitary"). One product team builds a system for itself, and others borrow it. It's fast and grounded in real work, but it naturally bends towards that one team's needs, so other teams often don't adopt it.

A central team ("centralised"). A dedicated team builds and runs the system for everyone. Quality and focus are high, but the team can drift away from what products actually need, and it can become a queue everyone is stuck in.

Shared ownership ("federated"). Designers and engineers across product teams share the work, with no central team. It sounds fair and efficient. In practice it's often nobody's real job, so it quietly stalls. Curtis himself later concluded that successful systems always have a central team, and also seek participation from a wider community.[[c:curtis-fallacy]]

Central team plus contributors (a hybrid). A core team owns quality and direction, and product teams contribute improvements back. Salesforce described their version as "cyclical": the core and the contributors constantly feed each other.[[c:salesforce-cyclical]] This is where most mature systems end up.

Real-world exampleSpotify: from 22 systems to one family
What happened

After Spotify's original central design system team was disbanded, teams started building their own. By the time the company looked again, it had 22 separate design systems.

The problem

Each one made sense locally, but together they made the product inconsistent and duplicated a lot of work.

What they did

They created Encore, a "system of systems": shared foundations at the core, with local systems for specific products or platforms on top.[[c:spotify-encore]]

[[prac]] Our recommendation

Start with one team or a central team while you get the basics right. As soon as two or more teams depend on the system, move to "central team plus contributors", and do it on purpose: set aside contributor time in product teams' plans instead of hoping people will help in their spare time.

05.3Who decides what goes in?

Imagine two product teams. The payments team needs a date picker that shows bank holidays. The reporting team needs a date picker that selects ranges of months. Both ask the system team for "a date picker". Who decides what gets built, and does it go into the shared system at all?

This is the everyday reality of running a system, and the answer starts with a simple idea: not everything belongs in the core.

In our date picker example, the system team builds (or improves) a basic, accessible date picker in the core. The reporting team builds a month-range picker as an extension, using core parts. The payments team adds bank holidays locally. Everyone gets what they need, the core stays clean, and if a third team later needs month ranges, the extension is a strong candidate to move into the core.

Write down who decides what

Most arguments about design systems are really arguments about who gets to decide. You can prevent most of them by writing it down in advance. Here's a starting point for a central team with contributors:

DecisionWho makes the callWho should be asked first
Changing a foundation (colours, text sizes)System leadProduct designers, brand, engineering
Adding a new component to the coreSystem leadThe teams that asked for it
Adding a variation to an existing componentSystem designerThe requesting team
How a component works in code, and changes that would break thingsSystem engineersProduct engineers
Whether a component meets accessibility rulesSystem lead (with an accessibility specialist)—
Building a local, product-only componentThe product teamSystem designer (a quick heads-up)
Removing an old componentSystem leadEvery team still using it
The system's roadmap and budgetDesign and engineering leadershipSystem lead, product teams

Look at the sixth row. Product teams are allowed to build their own components when the system doesn't cover what they need. A healthy system doesn't forbid that. It just asks teams to say so, so the system can learn from it.

05.4From "we need this" to "it's in the system"

Let's follow a real request through the system. Priya is a product designer on the billing team. She's designing a new invoice screen and needs a way to show a short list of key facts: amount, due date, status. Nothing in the system quite fits.

What happens next decides whether Priya trusts the system or quietly gives up on it. Brad Frost has described a governance process that many teams use as a starting point,[[c:frost-governance]] and here's our version of it, in plain steps.

  1. Check the system first

    Priya searches the system and its documentation. If something fits, she uses it. Done.

  2. Talk to the system team early

    Nothing fits, so she posts in the system's Slack channel with a screenshot and a sentence about the problem. Not a finished design, just the need. Early is cheap; late is expensive.

  3. Decide: one-off or shared?

    The system designer replies within a day or two. Have other teams needed this? It turns out the subscriptions team built something very similar last month. Two teams need it, so it's a candidate for the system.

  4. Agree what "good" looks like

    They agree on a rough scope together: which states it needs, how it behaves on small screens, and accessibility requirements.

  5. Build it once, properly

    Either the system team builds it, or Priya's team builds it with the system team's help (that's a contribution; see 05.5). Either way it gets designed for every state, built in code, tested and documented.

  6. Review, release and announce

    A final check, then it ships in the next update with a short note: what it is, when to use it, and a thank-you to Priya's team.

  7. Teams pick it up

    Billing and subscriptions both update and use the shared version. The subscriptions team's one-off copy is retired.

Meanwhile, Priya doesn't wait. If her deadline is before step 6, she builds a local version from system parts and swaps it for the official one later. The system should never be the reason a product team misses a deadline.

How do you decide what makes the cut?

The UK government's GOV.UK Design System publishes its criteria openly, and they're a great model. To be accepted, a proposed component or pattern must be useful (there's evidence other teams need it) and unique (it doesn't duplicate something that exists). Before it's published, it's checked to be usable (tested with real users, including disabled users), consistent (built from existing styles and parts) and versatile (works across browsers, devices and assistive technology).[[c:govuk-criteria]] [[doc]] You can adopt those five words almost as they are.

[[prac]] The rule of two

Our simplest test: something goes into the core when at least two teams need it. Until then, it lives locally with the team that built it, made from system parts so it's easy to promote later. This keeps the core free of one-offs and gives the system a steady supply of tried-and-tested candidates.

05.5Contributions: letting product teams help

A central team of three can't keep up with the needs of fifteen product teams. Contribution, meaning product teams improving the system themselves, is how small system teams serve big organisations. But it only works if contributing is easy and the effort matches the size of the change.

Nathan Curtis makes a useful distinction between small and large contributions.[[c:curtis-contributions]] Small ones are things like fixing a bug, improving an accessibility issue or correcting documentation: changes that are clearly improvements, which should go through a normal, quick review. Large ones change how something works or add something new, and they deserve a proper proposal and discussion before anyone builds them.

ScenarioThe engineer who fixed a bug and never contributed again
The situation

Arjun, a product engineer, finds that the system's dropdown can't be closed with the Escape key. He fixes it in an afternoon and submits the change.

What goes wrong

The change sits for three weeks waiting for a design review and a "contribution committee" meeting. By the time it's merged, Arjun has worked around it in his own code. He doesn't bother next time.

What should happen

A clear bug fix is a small contribution: one engineer reviews it within a few days, it ships in the next patch, and Arjun gets a thank-you in the release notes.

Proposals for bigger changes

New wordRFC"Request for comments": a short written proposal shared for feedback before a big decision is made.

For larger changes, many teams use a short written proposal, often called an RFC. Keep it to a page: what problem are we solving, what are the options, what do we recommend, and what would teams have to change? Share it for comments for a fixed time, say a week, and then the person responsible decides. Written proposals sound bureaucratic, but they're actually faster than meetings, because people can comment when it suits them and the reasoning is saved for later.

One last thought from Curtis that's worth holding on to: he has said he dislikes the word "contribution" because it makes teams focus on the output (a pull request) instead of the outcome (a better system for everyone).[[c:curtis-interview]] The point isn't to collect contributions. It's to make the system better and to make product teams feel it's theirs.

05.6Shipping changes without breaking anything

Here's a situation that happens in almost every design system eventually. The system team decides buttons should have slightly more rounded corners and a little more padding. It's a small visual tweak. They ship it on a Friday. On Monday, three product teams report broken screens: buttons no longer fit in a table row, a toolbar wraps onto two lines, and a checkout button now pushes the price off the screen on small phones.

Nothing in the code "broke". But the products did. Nathan Curtis wrote a whole article about exactly this: changes to colour, size and spacing can break a product as surely as a change to the code.[[c:curtis-visual-breaking]]

Version numbers, in plain words

New wordBreaking changeAn update that could make something stop working or look wrong in a product, so teams need to check or change their own work.

Most systems label their updates with a three-part version number, like 4.2.1, following a widely used convention called semantic versioning.[[c:semver]] It's simpler than it sounds. Each number tells teams how careful they need to be when updating:

Which number changesWhat it means for teamsDesign system examples
First (4 → 5)"Careful: you'll need to check or change your work."A component renamed or removed; a named colour removed; button sizes changed in a way that affects layouts.
Middle (4.2 → 4.3)"New things added; nothing you use should change."A new component, a new variation, a new icon.
Last (4.2.1 → 4.2.2)"Fixes only. Safe to update."A bug fix; an accessibility fix that doesn't change size or layout; a docs correction.

Curtis's series on releasing design systems goes into this in depth: how often to release, how to number versions, and how to handle breaking changes.[[c:curtis-releasing]] The headline advice is simple. Decide in advance which kinds of visual change count as breaking, write it down, and stick to it. In our button story, a change to padding that affects layouts should have been a "first number" release, announced in advance, with screenshots and a test period.

Retiring old things gracefully

New wordDeprecationMarking something as "old, please stop using it" before actually removing it, so teams have time to switch.

Sooner or later you'll need to replace a component. Doing it suddenly breaks things; never doing it leaves a mess. The healthy middle path looks like this. First, mark the old component as deprecated: it still works, but the documentation and a warning in the code say "please use the new one". Then give teams a guide to switching, and, where the change is mechanical, an automated script to do it for them. Track who's still using the old version. Only remove it in the next "first number" release, after a notice period everyone knows about in advance.

Telling people what changed

Release notes are the system's most-read documentation, so write them for the people using the system, not for the people who built it. Compare "Refactored Button padding tokens" with "Buttons are now 4px taller. If you've placed buttons inside table rows, check they still fit." The second one actually helps someone. Add before-and-after screenshots for anything visual. For bigger releases, a ten-minute demo in a team meeting goes a long way.

05.7Getting people to actually use it

Here's an uncomfortable truth: a design system nobody uses is just an expensive side project. Adoption is the system team's most important job, and Ben Callahan's maturity model puts "growing adoption" right after "building version one" for good reason.[[c:callahan-maturity]] It's the stage where many systems stall.

The good news is that adoption is rarely about persuasion. It's about making the system the easiest way to get work done. Teams take the path of least resistance. Your job is to make sure that path runs through the system.

Start with a pilot

Dan Mall, in his book Design That Scales, recommends building the system through pilots: real product projects where the system is used, tested and improved at the same time as the product work gets done.[[c:mall-dts]] Like a TV pilot, it's a low-risk way to find out what works before you roll it out widely. It also gives you your first success story: a real team that shipped faster, which is far more convincing than any slide.

Scenario"I couldn't find it, so I made my own"
The situation

A new designer joins and needs a warning banner. The Figma library has 400 components with names like "Alert_v2_final" and "Banner/New/Warning (old)".

What goes wrong

She can't tell which one is current, so she draws her own. Engineering builds it from scratch. The product now has a fourth kind of warning.

What fixes it

Clear names, removing or hiding old versions, a search-friendly description on every component, and a "start here" page. Findability is adoption. Part 03 covers library organisation in detail.

What works

Great documentation

Teams use what they can understand in five minutes before a deadline. Layer One's rule: docs decide adoption.

Champions in each team

One designer and one engineer per product team who get early previews and a direct line to the system team.

Office hours

A weekly slot where anyone can bring a screen and get help building it with the system.

Starter templates

New projects begin with the system already set up, so using it is the default, not an extra step.

Automatic nudges

Code checks that flag hard-coded colours or old components during code review.

Share the credit

Celebrate contributors and pilot teams in release notes and all-hands meetings.

A mistake we see often

Mandating adoption before earning it

"From next quarter, all teams must use the design system." If the system isn't ready to cover their needs, teams will comply on paper and work around it in practice, and they'll resent it. Make it genuinely useful first; mandates work best when they confirm something teams already want.

05.8Keeping it healthy for years

Design systems rarely die suddenly. They fade. Updates get less frequent, requests pile up, the Figma library and the code drift apart, and one day someone says "we should build a new design system", and the cycle starts again.

You can see it coming if you check a few simple signals regularly. Review them with the team monthly, and with leadership every quarter. Look at which way they're moving, not at hitting exact targets.

SignalWhat to look atHealthyWarning sign
UsageShare of UI built with the system; how many products are on a recent versionRising; most products within one major versionFlat; products stuck on old versions
CoverageHard-coded values in code; local components per productHard-coded values fallingLocal components growing faster than the core
QualityOpen bugs; accessibility errorsBugs fixed faster than they arriveThe same kind of bug keeps coming back
SpeedTime from request to release; number of contributionsDays to weeks; contributions growingMonths; contributions dropping to zero
Feeling"The system makes my work faster" (% who agree)Most agree, and risingPeople call it a blocker

Tidy as you go

New wordDesign debtShortcuts and inconsistencies that pile up over time and make future work slower, like clutter in a garage.

Every system builds up clutter: components that no longer quite fit, workarounds that became permanent, copies that teams made and modified, old versions still in use, and places where the Figma library and the code no longer match. That's normal. The problem is clutter nobody can see. Keep a simple, visible list of it (a "debt register"), and set aside a fixed slice of every cycle, around a fifth of the team's time, to clear some of it. [[prac]] Teams that tidy a little all the time rarely need a big rewrite.

Watch your own capacity

Finally, look after the team. In zeroheight's 2025 survey, 41% of respondents said they didn't have enough people to run their system, and that was most common at smaller companies.[[c:zeroheight-2025]] [[doc]] An overstretched system team becomes slow, a slow team becomes a bottleneck, and a bottleneck gets worked around. Protecting the team's capacity is part of protecting the system.

Operating checklist

  • Ownership model chosen and written down
  • Who-decides-what table agreed (05.3)
  • A clear path from request to release (05.4)
  • Contribution levels published, with quick reviews for small fixes
  • Core, extensions and local levels defined
  • Versioning rules, including which visual changes are "breaking"
  • A deprecation process with a notice period
  • Release notes written for the people using the system
  • A pilot team and champions in each product team
  • A monthly health check and a visible debt list

The short version

What to remember from this chapter

  1. Treat the system as a product with users, a roadmap, releases and support, not a project with an end date.
  2. Most mature systems end up with a central team plus contributors from product teams. Plan contributor time on purpose.
  3. Not everything belongs in the core. Use levels (core, extensions, local) and the rule of two.
  4. Decide in advance which visual changes count as breaking, and announce changes in plain language.
  5. Adoption is the main job: pilot, document well, and make the system the easiest path.
